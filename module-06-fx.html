01 · No issuer, no maturity, no ownership claim

What Is FX? — Theory & Terminology

FX (Foreign Exchange) is the exchange of one currency for another at an agreed rate. There is no issuer, no maturity in the bond sense, no ownership claim — just two counterparties agreeing that one currency is worth a certain amount of the other, right now or on some future date. FX is the largest financial market in the world by daily turnover, and unlike Equity or Fixed Income, every FX product is fundamentally a contract to exchange currency.

TermMeaning
Currency PairTwo currencies quoted against each other, e.g. USD/CAD — the first is the base currency, the second is the quote currency
Exchange RateHow much of the quote currency one unit of the base currency costs — e.g. USD/CAD = 1.3500 means 1 USD costs CAD 1.35
Spot RateToday's exchange rate, for near-immediate settlement (Module 1 §6)
Forward PointsThe adjustment added to (or subtracted from) the spot rate to get a forward rate, driven by the interest rate differential between the two currencies (Module 2 §10)
NotionalThe currency amount referenced by the contract — in FX, this genuinely does settle for Spot/Forward (unlike an IRS's notional, Module 1 §8)
Takeaway. FX sits in an unusual spot in the Asset Class Roadmap — it has no "security" the way Equity (a share) or Fixed Income (a bond) does. Every single FX product, from the simplest same-day trade to the most complex option, is a contractual agreement between two parties. Section 03 makes this explicit.

02 · Same mechanic, different points on a timeline

What Are The Products? — Spot, Forward, Swap & Options

Three of the four products are really the same underlying mechanic — exchanging currency at an agreed rate on an agreed date — differing only in when that date is and how many exchanges happen.

Diagram · FX Product TreeBy timing & obligation
FX
│
├── FX Spot       (near-immediate exchange, T+1/T+2 per currency pair)
├── FX Forward     (exchange on a later agreed date, rate fixed today — Module 2 §10)
├── FX Swap        (a spot leg + a forward leg, opposite directions)
└── FX Options     (the RIGHT, not obligation, to exchange at a fixed rate)
ProductWhat it is
FX SpotAn agreement to exchange two currencies almost immediately — T+1 for most pairs, T+2 for a few (Module 1 §6)
FX ForwardAn agreement to exchange two currencies on a specified future date, at a rate fixed today (Module 2 §10's worked example)
FX SwapTwo linked exchanges in opposite directions — a "near leg" (usually spot) and a "far leg" (a forward) — economically a short-term currency-denominated loan, not a long-dated interest rate product
FX OptionA contract giving the buyer the right, not the obligation, to exchange currency at a fixed strike rate by/at expiry, for a premium
A naming trap: "FX Swap" is not the same thing as a Cross-Currency Swap — a longer-dated product with periodic interest payments exchanged in two currencies over years, which behaves much more like the Interest Rate Swap from Module 1 §8 and belongs properly to Module 8 (Rates). An FX Swap is short-dated (commonly overnight to one year) and has no periodic interest payments at all — just two exchanges of principal.
Takeaway. Three of these four products (Spot, Forward, Swap) are really the same underlying mechanic — exchanging currency at an agreed rate on an agreed date — differing only in when that date is and how many exchanges happen. The Option is the odd one out: a contingent right, not a firm obligation, exactly the same buyer/seller asymmetry Module 4 §4d established for equity options.

03 · No security to contrast against

What Exactly Is Being Traded? — Contracts, All The Way Down

Unlike Module 4 (Equity: security vs. contract) or Module 5 (Fixed Income: security vs. financing contract), FX has no security to contrast against at all. Every product in Section 02 is a contract — Spot and Forward are the same mechanic at different points on a timeline, not a "real thing" vs. a "derivative of a real thing."

Diagram · FX Products, All ContractsNo security equivalent
FX Spot:     an agreement to exchange currencies almost immediately
             (T+1/T+2, per currency pair) -- the shortest-dated
             point on the timeline

FX Forward:  an agreement to exchange currencies on a LATER agreed
             date, at a rate fixed TODAY -- same mechanic as Spot,
             just further out on the same timeline

FX Swap:     TWO linked exchanges in opposite directions (near leg
             + far leg) -- effectively a short-term FX-funding
             transaction, not a new mechanic

FX Option:   a RIGHT (not obligation) to exchange currencies at a
             fixed rate, for a premium -- the one genuinely
             different mechanic in the group

-> Unlike Equity (Module 4) or Fixed Income (Module 5), there is
   no underlying "security" any of these four reference. FX is
   contracts all the way down — even Spot is just the shortest-
   dated point on the same continuum as Forward.
Takeaway. This reframes "settlement" for FX entirely — Module 1 §6 already showed FX Spot settling T+1/T+2 like a security would, but it never was one. What actually happens at settlement (Section 05) is two currency payments exchanging hands, not a security moving from a seller's CSD account to a buyer's.

04 · Buyer → Seller → Trade

Real-World Trade Examples

Four trades — Spot, Forward, Swap, and an Option hedge — all in USD/CAD.

4a. FX Spot

FX Spot · USD/CADT+1 settlement
Buyer:  Bank Of Toronto (buying USD)
Seller: Goldman Sachs

Trade: Bank Of Toronto buys USD 10,000,000 @ Spot Rate 1.3500
Bank Of Toronto pays  = 10,000,000 x 1.3500 = CAD 13,500,000
Bank Of Toronto receives = USD 10,000,000
Settlement: T+1 (Module 1 §6 — USD/CAD is one of the pairs that
            settles T+1 rather than the more common T+2)
Schema: FxTrade.avsc / FxValuation.avsc — the taxonomy's single "FX" node covers Spot, Forward, and Swap alike (Section 02); only FX Option and the hybrid FX/IR Exotic get their own schemas.

4b. FX Forward

FX Forward · USD/CAD, 3-MonthReused from Module 2 §10
Today's Spot Rate (USD/CAD): 1.3500
3-Month Forward Points:      +0.0045
3-Month Forward Rate:        1.3545

Bank Of Toronto agrees TODAY with Goldman Sachs:
  In 3 months, pays CAD 13,545,000, receives USD 10,000,000
  (10,000,000 x 1.3545 = 13,545,000)
-> No cash moves today. Only the RATE is locked in now.
Schema: FxTrade.avsc / FxValuation.avsc — same schema as Spot; the forward date and forward points are fields on the same trade schema, not a separate product.

4c. FX Swap

FX Swap · Near Leg + Far Leg1-month funding
NEAR LEG (today, effectively FX Spot):
  Bank Of Toronto buys USD 10,000,000 @ Spot Rate 1.3500
  Pays CAD 13,500,000 today, receives USD 10,000,000 today

FAR LEG (in 1 month, a Forward in the opposite direction):
  Bank Of Toronto sells USD 10,000,000 @ Forward Rate 1.3512
  (1-month forward points +0.0012, reflecting the CAD/USD
  interest rate differential over that month)
  Receives CAD 13,512,000 in 1 month, pays back USD 10,000,000

NET EFFECT:
  Bank Of Toronto had the use of USD 10,000,000 for exactly one
  month, and the two legs together cost:
    13,512,000 - 13,500,000 = CAD 12,000
  -> this CAD 12,000 is effectively the FUNDING COST of borrowing
     USD for a month via FX markets, priced entirely through the
     forward points — no separate "interest payment" line item
     the way a loan or a Cross-Currency Swap would have.
Schema: FxTrade.avsc / FxValuation.avsc — still the same schema, modeled as two linked legs on one trade record. Do not confuse this with FxIRExoticTrade.avsc — that schema is for the longer-dated Cross-Currency Swap flagged just above, which behaves like an IRS in two currencies and belongs to the "FX/IR Exotic" node, not plain "FX."

4d. FX Option

FX Option · USD Call / CAD PutClient hedge, Strike 1.3600
Buyer: Bank Of Toronto's client (needs to buy USD in 3 months)
Seller (writer): Bank Of Toronto

Trade: USD Call / CAD Put, Notional USD 5,000,000
       Strike 1.3600 CAD/USD, Expiry 3 months
       Premium: 0.0180 CAD per USD notional
Premium Paid = 5,000,000 x 0.0180 = CAD 90,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario A: Spot moves to 1.3800 (USD stronger)
  Option is in-the-money: exercising saves
    (1.3800 - 1.3600) x 5,000,000 = CAD 100,000 vs. buying at spot
  Net Benefit = 100,000 - 90,000 (premium) = CAD 10,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario B: Spot moves to 1.3400 (USD weaker)
  Option expires WORTHLESS — buying USD at spot (1.3400) is
  cheaper than exercising at strike (1.3600)
  Maximum Loss = the CAD 90,000 premium, exactly as Module 4 §4d
  established for the AAPL call — never more than premium paid.
Schema: FxOptionTrade.avsc / FxOptionValuation.avsc — this OTC, bilaterally-negotiated hedge (Section 07 confirms FX Options are mostly bilateral) gets its own dedicated schema, distinct from plain FxTrade.avsc.
Takeaway. Notice the FX Swap (4c) is really just a Spot (4a) and a Forward (4b) bolted together — nothing new mechanically, just two of the same product executed simultaneously in opposite directions. The Option (4d) is the only one of the four with the asymmetric payoff Module 4 already established for equity options.

CLS — FX's answer to DvP

Module 1 §7 established DvP (Delivery versus Payment) as the mechanism that ties a security's two settlement legs together so neither side is ever left exposed. FX has no security to deliver — instead, CLS (Continuous Linked Settlement), owned and used by the major global banks (including, illustratively, Bank Of Toronto and Goldman Sachs), performs the FX equivalent: PvP (Payment versus Payment).

Diagram · PvP via CLSWhy Herstatt risk doesn't happen
WITHOUT PvP (the old failure mode, named after Bankhaus Herstatt's
1974 collapse):
  Bank Of Toronto pays CAD 13,500,000 in Toronto's business hours.
  Goldman Sachs is due to pay USD 10,000,000 in New York hours,
  LATER THE SAME DAY. If Goldman Sachs fails in between, Bank Of
  Toronto has paid and received nothing back.

WITH CLS (PvP):
  Both legs are settled simultaneously across CLS's own books,
  covering 18+ major currencies including USD and CAD. Either
  BOTH payments complete, or NEITHER does — mirroring exactly what
  DvP does for securities (Module 1 §7), just for two currency
  payments instead of a security + cash.
Takeaway. CLS is not a CCP — it doesn't novate trades or take on counterparty credit risk the way NSCC or a swap CCP does (Module 3 §6). It's pure settlement-risk mitigation, the FX-specific answer to the exact problem DvP solves for securities.

06 · No ISIN, no CUSIP, no Ticker

Identifiers — Currency Pairs, UPI, UTI & LEI

FX mostly doesn't use the identifiers Modules 4 and 5 relied on — there's no ISIN, CUSIP, SEDOL, Ticker, or FIGI for a currency pair trade, because (Section 03) there's no security to identify.

IdentifierApplies to FX?Example
ISIN / CUSIP / SEDOL / Ticker / FIGINo — these identify securities; FX has none—
Currency Pair (ISO 4217 codes)Yes — the closest thing FX has to an "instrument" identifierUSD/CAD
UPIYes, for FX Forwards, Swaps, and Options reported as OTC derivatives (Module 2 §12)One UPI for "USD/CAD, Forward, Deliverable" — reused across many Forward trades, same logic as Module 2 §12's IRS example
UTIYes — every individual FX Forward/Swap/Option trade gets its ownA fresh UTI per trade, same as Module 2 §12
LEIYes — identifies Bank Of Toronto, Goldman Sachs, and any client as counterpartiesEntity identifier, not instrument (Module 4 §6)
Takeaway. If a reporting record shows a currency pair but no ISIN/CUSIP, that's completely normal for FX — the absence of a security identifier isn't a data gap, it's the correct state for an asset class that has no security at all (Section 03).

07 · A third mechanism neither Equity nor Fixed Income has

Exchange vs OTC vs Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared

The overwhelming majority of FX trades — Spot, Forward, Swap, and most Options — are OTC and bilateral, settled via CLS's PvP mechanism rather than novated to a CCP. A smaller but growing share of Forwards and NDFs clear centrally, and listed FX futures/options exist on exchange as a separate, smaller market.

ProductExchange or OTC?Bilateral or Centrally Cleared?
FX SpotOTCBilateral (settled via CLS's PvP, not a CCP — Section 05)
FX ForwardOTCMostly bilateral; a growing share clears via LCH ForexClear or CME (especially NDFs)
FX SwapOTCBilateral (the norm)
FX OptionOTCMostly bilateral
FX Futures/Options (listed)Exchange-traded (e.g. CME)Centrally cleared — a smaller, separate market from OTC FX, not in this module's four core products but worth knowing exists
Takeaway. FX is structurally closer to Module 5's Fixed Income (bilateral OTC as the default) than to Module 4's Equity (centrally cleared as the default) — but FX adds a third mechanism, CLS's PvP, that neither of those two asset classes has: settlement-risk mitigation without a CCP's counterparty-risk mitigation. Bilateral counterparty risk (Module 3 §6) and settlement risk (this module) are two different risks, and CLS only solves the second one.

08 · A currency-peg defender, not just a supervisor

Regulatory Perspective — MiFID, EMIR, SFTR, FCA, CIRO, HKMA, DTCC, DMO

HKMA shows up in a genuinely different role here than in Equity or Fixed Income — and the Spot/Forward boundary is exactly where EMIR's scope begins.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches FX specifically
MiFID II / MiFIR (EU)Covers FX Forwards, Swaps, and Options used for investment purposes; physically-settled FX Forwards used purely for commercial/payment purposes often sit outside MiFID's scope — a genuinely contested boundary in EU rulemaking
EMIRApplies to FX Forwards, Swaps, and Options as OTC derivatives (Module 2 §07) — but not to FX Spot. The boundary between "Spot" and "Forward" is defined by a settlement-date threshold (roughly the standard spot cycle for that currency pair, T+1/T+2 for most, longer for some emerging-market pairs) — anything settling within that window is Spot and out of scope; anything beyond it is a Forward and in scope
SFTRDoes not apply to FX Swaps, despite the name similarity to "swap" in a repo/securities-lending sense (Module 5 §08) — SFTR is specifically for securities/commodities financing, not currency exchange. A genuinely easy mix-up worth flagging explicitly
FCA (UK)Outsized relevance for FX specifically — London handles roughly 40%+ of global FX trading volume (BIS Triennial Survey), making the FCA one of the most consequential FX conduct regulators globally, not just a regional one
CIRO (Canada)SRO overseeing Canadian FX dealer conduct, alongside the Bank of Canada's broader market-infrastructure oversight
HKMA (Hong Kong)A third distinct role across this roadmap: for FX specifically, the HKMA directly intervenes in the market to defend the Hong Kong Dollar's peg to the US Dollar under the Linked Exchange Rate System — a direct market-participant role, not just a supervisory one
DTCCDTCC's GTR (Global Trade Repository) accepts FX derivative reports (Forwards, Swaps, Options) alongside other asset classes, feeding CFTC reporting requirements (systems-landscape §05)
DMO (UK Debt Management Office)Not FX-relevant — issues Gilts (Module 5 §08), included here for completeness only
Takeaway. HKMA has now shown up in a meaningfully different role in all three asset-class modules so far — a securities conduct footnote in Equity (Module 4), a direct bond issuer in Fixed Income (Module 5), and now a direct market participant defending a currency peg in FX. Same institution, three genuinely different jobs depending on the asset class in front of you.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Far-leg forward rate = 1.3480. Bank Of Toronto's funding cost would flip to a GAIN, not a cost
Math · Far Leg, Negative Forward PointsUSD/CAD Swap
Far leg: sell USD 10,000,000 @ 1.3480, receive CAD 13,480,000
Net effect = 13,480,000 - 13,500,000 = -CAD 20,000

Bank Of Toronto would receive CAD 20,000 less than it paid on the near leg — meaning it actually profits CAD 20,000 from holding the swap rather than paying a funding cost, because negative forward points (CAD getting cheaper forward, or equivalently a swap point flip) work in the opposite direction from the original +0.0012 example. The sign of the forward points, driven by the CAD/USD rate differential, directly determines whether the FX Swap is a net cost or a net benefit to whoever's borrowing the foreign currency.

Q2 — FX Spot has no such identifier because it has no security to identify at all (Section 03) — this is expected behavior, not a gap

Section 06's takeaway makes this explicit: ISIN/CUSIP/Ticker exist specifically to identify securities (Module 2 §2, Module 4 §6, Module 5 §6). FX products, including Spot, aren't securities — they're contracts to exchange currency, so the absence of a security identifier is exactly correct, not a missing field.

Q3 — That's exactly what a correctly-populated FX Forward trade record should look like

Section 06's table states this directly: UPI and UTI both apply to FX Forwards, Swaps, and Options (as OTC derivatives, Module 2 §12), while ISIN never applies to FX at all. A record with UPI/UTI populated and ISIN blank is the healthy pattern for FX, not a red flag — this contrasts with Module 5 §6's warning about the opposite pattern (UPI populated on a bond record being suspicious).

Q4 — CLS only solves settlement-timing risk (PvP) — it never becomes a party to the trade and doesn't absorb counterparty credit risk the way a CCP's novation does

Section 05's takeaway states this precisely: CLS mirrors DvP's mechanical protection (Module 1 §7) but stops there — it isn't a counterparty risk mitigant like the CCP novation described in Module 3 §6, just a settlement-risk mitigant.

Q5 — The option expires exactly at-the-money — worthless to exercise, so the buyer loses the full CAD 90,000 premium, the same maximum-loss outcome as Scenario B

At spot = strike = 1.3600, there is zero benefit to exercising (buying at 1.3600 via the option vs. buying at 1.3600 in the spot market is identical), so the CAD 90,000 premium is a pure loss — exactly Scenario B's outcome even though spot didn't fall all the way to 1.3400. This illustrates that an option's payoff has a hard breakeven at strike-plus-premium-equivalent, not a smooth scaling from Scenario B up to Scenario A.

Q6 — No, the FX Spot trade is not reportable under EMIR. The FX Forward from Section 04b IS reportable under EMIR

Section 08's table states this directly: EMIR excludes FX Spot by definition (it's outside the settlement-date threshold that defines a "derivative" for FX purposes) but explicitly covers FX Forwards, Swaps, and Options. The Section 04b Forward, settling 3 months out, is squarely inside EMIR's scope; the Section 04a Spot, settling T+1, is not.

Q7 — London (and therefore the FCA's jurisdiction) handles roughly 40%+ of ALL global FX trading volume, a scale that makes FCA oversight consequential to the entire global FX market
Section 08 flags this explicitly as "outsized relevance" — this is a genuinely different kind of importance than the conduct-regulator role CIRO or the FCA itself plays for, say, domestic equity trading (Module 4 §8), where each regulator's importance is roughly proportional to its own domestic market size, whereas CIRO's remit is scoped to Canadian dealer conduct specifically.
