01 · Not synonyms, on any well-built trade record

What Is Valuation? — Price, Market Value, Fair Value, MTM, PV & Exposure

Six terms that sound interchangeable in casual conversation mean six genuinely different things on a trade record, and confusing them is the single most common valuation mistake a reporting system can make.

TermMeaning
PriceThe per-unit quote for an instrument — CAD 68.40 for one Coca-Cola share (Module 4 §4a), 98.50 for one Canada bond per 100 face (Module 5 §4a). Static at a point in time, not multiplied by quantity yet
Market ValuePrice × Quantity — the total worth of a position right now, at today's Price
Fair ValueThe accounting-standard concept of Market Value, formalized with a specific measurement hierarchy (Section 06) — the number that actually lands in financial statements
MTM (Mark-to-Market)The act of revaluing a position to current Market/Fair Value, and by extension the resulting Unrealized P&L versus cost or the prior valuation date
PV (Present Value)The value today of a cash flow (or stream of cash flows) that will actually happen in the future, discounted back at an appropriate rate — the mechanism behind every derivative's valuation (Section 04)
ExposureHow much could be lost if things go wrong — NOT the same as Market Value. A CAD 100,000,000 notional IRS might have a Market Value near zero at inception but a very real, positive Potential Future Exposure (Module 11)
Diagram · Six Numbers, One Trade RecordCanada 5% Bond 2035, one week later
Price          : 98.65                (today's per-100 quote)
Market Value    : CAD 9,865,000        (98.65/100 x 10,000,000 face)
Fair Value      : CAD 9,865,000        (same number, IFRS 13/ASC 820
                   framing -- Section 06 explains WHY it's the same
                   number here but often ISN'T for a derivative)
MTM / Unrealized P&L : +CAD 15,000    (Market Value today minus
                   Market Value at purchase -- Module 5 §5's exact
                   worked example)
PV of remaining cash flows : NOT the same as Market Value for a
                   bond quoted on a clean-price convention -- see
                   Section 03's YTM/Duration deep dive
Exposure        : depends entirely on WHO holds the risk and
                   against WHOM -- covered fully in Module 11
Takeaway. Every worked example across Modules 4–9 and 15 already used at least two or three of these six terms without formally separating them. This module's whole job is to make that separation explicit, then show the actual math — YTM/Duration for Fixed Income (Section 03), DCF/NPV and option pricing for derivatives (Section 04) — that every prior module deferred to "Module 10."

02 · Look it up, or calculate it

Two Valuation Families — Quote-Based vs Model-Based

Every instrument in this roadmap falls into exactly one of two valuation families, based on whether an observable market price exists for that exact contract.

Diagram · Quote-Based vs Model-BasedNot the same axis as Exchange vs OTC
QUOTE-BASED (read the price, multiply by quantity):
  Coca-Cola stock (Module 4 §4a)     -> NYSE prints a price every trade
  Canada 5% Bond 2035 (Module 5 §4a) -> dealers quote a Clean Price
  CME WTI Future (Module 7 §4b)      -> exchange publishes a daily
                                          settlement price
  E-mini S&P 500 Future (Module 15 §4a) -> same mechanic

MODEL-BASED (calculate a value from inputs, no single quote exists):
  5Y CAD IRS (Module 8 §4a)          -> no exchange prints "the"
                                          price of THIS specific swap;
                                          value comes from discounting
                                          its cash flows (Section 04a)
  AAPL Call Option (Module 4 §4d)    -> value comes from an option
                                          pricing model (Section 04b)
  CDS on Northstar Resources
  (Module 9 §4a)                     -> value comes from the credit
                                          spread curve (Section 04c)

-> The dividing line isn't "exchange-traded vs OTC" (Module 6 §7's
   axis) -- Listed Options ARE exchange-traded (Module 15 §7) but
   still need a MODEL to price, because a specific strike/expiry
   combination may not trade often enough to have its own live quote.
Takeaway. This is the single most useful lens for predicting how hard a given instrument is to value correctly — Quote-Based instruments are a lookup; Model-Based instruments are a calculation, with every input in that calculation being a potential source of disagreement between two parties (or two systems) valuing the exact same trade.

03 · Delivering on two deferred promises

Fixed Income Deep Dive — YTM and Duration/DV01, In Full

Module 5 §1 introduced Yield to Maturity and Duration as previews, promising the full math here. This section delivers both, using the exact Canada 5% Bond 2035 position from Module 5 §4a.

Yield to Maturity — the approximate formula

Math · Approximate YTMCanada 5% Bond 2035
Face Value (F): CAD 10,000,000
Clean Price Value (P): CAD 9,850,000 (Price 98.50 — Module 5 §4a)
Annual Coupon Cash Flow (C): 10,000,000 x 5% = CAD 500,000
Years to Maturity (n): 9 (2035 - 2026)

Approx. YTM = [ C + (F - P)/n ] / [ (F + P)/2 ]
            = [ 500,000 + (10,000,000 - 9,850,000)/9 ] / [ (10,000,000 + 9,850,000)/2 ]
            = [ 500,000 + 16,666.67 ] / 9,925,000
            = 516,666.67 / 9,925,000
            = 5.21%

-> YTM (5.21%) > Coupon Rate (5.00%), exactly the discount-bond
   rule Module 1 §1 established: paying below par means the buyer's
   actual return exceeds the printed coupon rate.

Modified Duration and DV01

Module 5 §1 previewed Modified Duration as "how much a bond's price moves for a 1% change in yield." Given this bond's actual 9-year, 5% semi-annual cash flow schedule at a 5.21% yield, its Modified Duration works out to approximately 7.2 years — the full Macaulay-Duration-to-Modified-Duration derivation is a standard fixed income calculation, not reproduced step-by-step here, but the consequence of that number is exactly what an IT/reporting system needs to get right:

Math · DV01Canada 5% Bond 2035
Modified Duration: 7.2
Market Value: CAD 9,850,000

DV01 = Modified Duration x Market Value x 0.0001
     = 7.2 x 9,850,000 x 0.0001
     = CAD 7,092 per basis point

SCENARIO — yields rise 25 basis points (0.25%):
  Approx. Price Impact = -Modified Duration x ΔYield x Market Value
                        = -7.2 x 0.0025 x 9,850,000
                        = -CAD 177,300

-> This is the SAME DV01 concept Module 8 §1 previewed for Rates
   products, applied here to the instrument it was originally
   defined for: bond price sensitivity. A 25bp rate move costs
   Bank Of Toronto's bond position roughly CAD 177,300 -- a real,
   quantifiable number a risk report needs, not just a directional
   "rates went up, bonds went down" statement.
Takeaway. YTM answers "what am I actually earning if I hold to maturity"; Duration/DV01 answers "how much does this position's value move if yields shift." Both are derived entirely from the same three inputs (Price, Coupon, Maturity) that Module 5 §1 already established as the bond's core fields — nothing new is needed, just the math those fields were always building toward.

04 · Same trades, seen mid-life

Derivative Valuation Deep Dive — DCF, Option Pricing & Credit Spreads

Three worked examples — one per Model-Based family — each revisiting a trade already introduced earlier in this roadmap, now shown mid-life rather than at inception.

4a. Interest Rate Swap — DCF/NPV Mark-to-Market

5Y CAD IRSMTM, 6 months after inception
At inception (Module 8 §4a): Bank Of Toronto pays FIXED 4.00%,
Notional CAD 100,000,000. A fairly-priced swap has NPV = 0 at
inception -- the fixed rate is SET so that the PV of the fixed
leg exactly equals the PV of the expected floating leg.

SIX MONTHS LATER: the market's quoted rate for a new 4.5-year CAD
swap has moved from 4.00% to 4.30% -- rates rose.

Bank Of Toronto is locked into paying 4.00% fixed, now BELOW the
4.30% market rate for the same remaining term -- a good deal, so
the swap now has POSITIVE value to Bank Of Toronto.

Approx. MTM Value (using the swap's own DV01, the same concept as
Section 03's bond DV01, just computed off the swap's cash flow
schedule instead of a bond's):
  Swap DV01 (approx, CAD 100,000,000 notional, 4.5Y remaining):
    ≈ CAD 41,000 per basis point
  Rate moved 30 basis points (4.00% -> 4.30%)
  Approx. MTM Gain = 41,000 x 30 = CAD 1,230,000 (to Bank Of Toronto)

-> A full DCF valuation discounts EVERY remaining fixed and
   floating cash flow off the current CORRA curve individually --
   the DV01 shortcut shown here is the same simplification real
   risk systems use for a quick sensitivity estimate between full
   revaluations, not a replacement for the full curve-based DCF.

4b. Equity Option — Intrinsic Value vs Time Value

AAPL 15-Jan-2027 250 CallSix months before expiry
At inception (Module 4 §4d): Strike USD 250, Premium USD 18.50/share
100 contracts x 100 shares/contract = 10,000 shares equivalent

SIX MONTHS LATER: AAPL trades at USD 260, option premium now
quoted at USD 22.00/share

Intrinsic Value = max(Spot - Strike, 0) x Shares
                 = max(260 - 250, 0) x 10,000
                 = 10 x 10,000 = USD 100,000

Market Value (current premium) = 22.00 x 10,000 = USD 220,000

Time Value = Market Value - Intrinsic Value
           = 220,000 - 100,000 = USD 120,000

-> Time Value exists because of everything an option pricing
   model (the industry standard is a Black-Scholes-family model)
   actually prices in beyond today's spot-vs-strike gap: time
   remaining to expiry, volatility, the risk-free rate, and
   dividends -- the five/six inputs an option pricing model needs
   that Section 02's "Quote-Based" instruments never require at all.
IT/reporting angle: a trade record showing an option's current Market Value with no Intrinsic-Value/Time-Value split is hiding the single most useful diagnostic for whether that value looks right — Intrinsic Value is a pure arithmetic check (spot vs. strike, no model needed) that either matches a floor under the Market Value or signals a stale/wrong price feed.

4c. Credit Default Swap — Spread-Driven Mark-to-Market

5Y CDS on Northstar Resources Inc.Bank Of Toronto vs Goldman Sachs · One year after inception
At inception (Module 9 §4a): Notional USD 10,000,000, Running
Coupon 100bps, market spread was 250bps, Bank Of Toronto (protection
buyer) paid Goldman Sachs (protection seller) an upfront of approx.
USD 675,000
  = (2.50% - 1.00%) x ~4.5 x 10,000,000

ONE YEAR LATER: Northstar Resources Inc.'s credit has deteriorated
further (still no formal Credit Event, Module 9 §4b) -- market
spread has WIDENED to 400bps. Remaining Risky Duration ≈ 4.0
(one year of the original 5 has passed)

Current Approx. Value = (Market Spread - Running Coupon) x Risky
                          Duration x Notional
                        = (4.00% - 1.00%) x 4.0 x 10,000,000
                        = 3.00% x 4.0 x 10,000,000
                        = USD 1,200,000

Unrealized Gain to Bank Of Toronto = 1,200,000 - 675,000 = USD 525,000

-> Bank Of Toronto's PROTECTION position gains value as Northstar
   Resources Inc.'s credit WORSENS (spread widens) -- the mirror
   image of a bond holder, who LOSES value as the same company's
   credit worsens. This is exactly why CDS makes an effective
   hedge against a bond position referencing the same Reference
   Entity (Module 9 §1) -- and exactly the "wrong-way risk"
   concept flagged in Module 9's Q4 answer, from the opposite
   side of the trade.
Callout — one snapshot among several. This is a single illustrative valuation snapshot: Northstar Resources Inc.'s spread widening from 250bps to 400bps. Module 11 §3 revalues this exact same trade under a different illustrative scenario — a spread that has instead NARROWED, leaving Bank Of Toronto's locked-in protection worth less than current market pricing would provide, an unrealized loss rather than a gain. The two sections aren't describing the same moment in time; they're two independent "what if the spread moves this way" scenarios applied to the same trade, each self-consistent on its own terms.
Takeaway. All three derivative valuations reduce to the same underlying idea — compare what was locked in at trade date against what the market offers today for the same remaining risk, then translate that gap into a dollar value using a sensitivity measure (Swap DV01, Option Greeks, CDS Spread DV01) that's structurally the same concept every time, just calibrated to a different underlying risk factor.

06 · Ranked by input observability, not asset class

The Fair Value Hierarchy — IFRS 13 / ASC 820 Levels 1, 2 & 3

Accounting standards classify every Fair Value measurement into one of three Levels, based entirely on how observable its inputs are — not on which asset class the instrument belongs to.

Diagram · Level 1, 2, 3Same product, different Level, per trade
LEVEL 1 — quoted price, identical instrument, active market:
  Coca-Cola stock (Module 4 §4a), CME WTI Future settlement price
  (Module 7 §5), E-mini S&P 500 Future (Module 15 §4a)
  -> the cleanest case: read the price, no judgment involved

LEVEL 2 — observable inputs, but not a direct quote for THIS
exact instrument:
  5Y CAD IRS (Module 8 §4a) -- no one quotes "Bank Of Toronto's
    exact swap," but the CORRA curve feeding its DCF is fully
    observable
  Canada 5% Bond 2035, if it trades infrequently -- valued off
    comparable, more liquid bonds' observable yields
  A liquid, standardized single-name CDS (Module 9 §2) -- market
    spread is observable even though no exchange quotes it directly

LEVEL 3 — unobservable inputs, model assumptions, genuine
judgment required:
  CDS on a THINLY-TRADED reference entity, where the "market
    spread" input itself has to be estimated, not observed
    (Module 9 §4a's Northstar Resources Inc. trade, if Northstar
    bonds/CDS rarely trade)
  A bespoke FX/IR Exotic (Module 6 §4c's naming-trap Cross-
    Currency Swap, or an exotic barrier option) with no comparable
    liquid market at all

-> Notice EQUITY, COMMODITY, and RATES products all show up in
   BOTH Level 1 and Level 2 depending on liquidity, not asset
   class -- the same instrument TYPE can sit at different Levels
   for two different specific trades.
Takeaway. The Fair Value Hierarchy is Section 02's Quote-Based/Model-Based split, refined one step further — Quote-Based instruments are always Level 1; Model-Based instruments split into Level 2 (observable model inputs) or Level 3 (unobservable ones), and that Level 2/3 split is exactly what determines how much independent scrutiny a valuation needs (Section 07).

07 · Three fields, three questions, one control function

IT/Reporting Angle — Which Field Is Which, and Who Checks It

Three fields that look similar on a trade record answer three different questions, and a Model-Based position needs a fourth process entirely: someone independent re-deriving the number to make sure it's right.

Diagram · Three Fields, Three QuestionsPrice / Market Value / Unrealized P&L
"Price" / "Rate" field   ->  What does the market say THIS
                               instrument is worth, per unit?

"Market Value" field     ->  What is my TOTAL position worth,
                               right now?

"Unrealized P&L" field   ->  How much has that total worth CHANGED
                               since I entered (or since the last
                               valuation date)?

-> A reporting system that only stores "Market Value" and expects
   a downstream consumer to infer Price or P&L by comparing rows
   over time is pushing work onto every consumer that the source
   system could have done once, correctly.

Independent Price Verification (IPV): for Level 2 and especially Level 3 positions (Section 06), the Front Office trader's own mark is never the last word — a separate function (commonly Product Control, inside Middle Office, Module 1 §4) independently re-derives the valuation, typically monthly, using its own sourced inputs, and any material difference from the trader's mark gets investigated and, if justified, adjusted. This is precisely why Level 3 positions (Module 9's illiquid single-name CDS, Module 6's bespoke Cross-Currency Swap) draw far more IPV scrutiny than a Level 1 Coca-Cola position ever would — there's no independent market quote to check a Level 1 mark against a second source; there's only a Level 3 mark's model and assumptions to check.

Takeaway. The distinction between Market Value and Exposure (Section 01) matters most here too — IPV exists to verify that today's valuation number is correct, a completely separate question from Module 11's concern about how much that valuation could still move against you before the next check.

08 · The first module led by accounting standard-setters

Regulatory Perspective — IFRS 13/ASC 820, Basel FRTB, EMIR/Dodd-Frank Valuation Reporting

Trading regulation meets financial-reporting regulation for the first time in this roadmap.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Valuation specifically
IFRS 13 (international)The global accounting standard defining Fair Value and its three-Level hierarchy (Section 06) — issued by the IASB (International Accounting Standards Board), the first standard-setter in this roadmap that isn't a markets/derivatives regulator at all, but an accounting one
ASC 820 (US)The US GAAP equivalent of IFRS 13, issued by the FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) — same three-Level concept, different standard-setter, the accounting-world parallel to Module 8 §08's CFTC/SEC or Module 9 §08's split-jurisdiction pattern, just for financial reporting rather than trading regulation
BCBS FRTBThe Basel Committee's post-2008 overhaul of how banks must calculate regulatory capital against trading-book market risk — directly built on the same Quote-Based/Model-Based and Level 1/2/3 distinctions this module established: FRTB explicitly penalizes banks with higher capital charges for holding positions with less-observable, harder-to-verify valuations
EMIR / Dodd-FrankBoth regimes require OTC derivative trades reported to a Trade Repository (Module 2 §07) to include periodic valuation/mark-to-market fields alongside the static trade economics — Section 04's worked examples are exactly the numbers those reporting fields are populated with
IOSCO / BCBS (joint)Their joint margin requirements for non-centrally-cleared derivatives (Module 12: Collateral & Margin) depend entirely on accurate, independently-verified valuations (Section 07's IPV) to calculate Initial and Variation Margin correctly
Takeaway. This module's regulatory section is the first in the roadmap dominated by accounting standard-setters (IASB, FASB) rather than markets regulators — a reminder that "Valuation" as a topic sits at the intersection of trading (why the number needs to exist at all) and financial reporting (why the number needs to be defensible on a balance sheet), two different disciplines this roadmap has kept mostly separate until now.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Approximate YTM ≈ 4.73%, sitting BELOW the 5.00% coupon rate
Math · YTM at a Premium PriceCanada 5% Bond 2035
C = 500,000, F = 10,000,000, P = 10,200,000 (Price 102.00), n = 9

Approx. YTM = [ 500,000 + (10,000,000 - 10,200,000)/9 ] / [ (10,000,000 + 10,200,000)/2 ]
            = [ 500,000 - 22,222.22 ] / 10,100,000
            = 477,777.78 / 10,100,000
            = 4.73%

Paying a premium (Price > Face Value) means the buyer's actual return falls below the printed coupon rate — the mirror image of Section 03's discount-bond result, and the exact rule Module 1 §1 established: Yield < Coupon Rate whenever Price > Par.

Q2 — Approximate price GAIN of CAD 283,680
Math · DV01, Yields FallCanada 5% Bond 2035
Price Impact = -DV01 x Basis Points Moved (with sign flipped for a FALL)
             = 7,092 x 40
             = CAD 283,680 gain

DV01 measures the price impact per basis point in either direction — Section 03's worked example showed a loss when yields ROSE 25bps; the same CAD 7,092/bp sensitivity produces a GAIN when yields FALL, simply because bond prices and yields move inversely (Module 1 §1's foundational price-vs-rates relationship).

Q3 — Intrinsic Value = USD 0. The Market Value could still be positive, entirely from Time Value

Intrinsic Value = max(245 - 250, 0) x 10,000 = max(-5, 0) x 10,000 = USD 0

An option's Intrinsic Value can never go negative — the buyer simply wouldn't exercise an out-of-the-money option, so its floor is zero, not a negative number (Module 4 §4d's Scenario B already established this: the option "expires WORTHLESS," never at a loss beyond the premium). But Section 04b's Time Value component (driven by time-to-expiry, volatility, etc.) doesn't depend on being in-the-money — a six-months-out option with real volatility can absolutely still command a positive premium even while USD 5 out-of-the-money, entirely as Time Value with zero Intrinsic Value.

Q4 — Bonds (Fixed Income): Quote-Based when actively traded, Level-2-Model-Based (via comparable bonds' yields) when illiquid

Section 05's table flags Fixed Income as having a "Quote-Based (Clean Price)" primary basis, but Section 06's Level 2 example explicitly uses "Canada 5% Bond 2035, if it trades infrequently" as valued off comparable liquid bonds instead — proving that Valuation Family (Section 02) isn't fixed per product type, it's fixed per how liquid that specific instrument is right now.

Q5 — More likely Level 2 — the market spread for a highly liquid, frequently-traded name is genuinely observable
Section 06 draws exactly this line: "a liquid, standardized single-name CDS... market spread is observable even though no exchange quotes it directly" is explicitly called out as a Level 2 example, contrasted directly against the Level 3 example of a CDS on a thinly-traded reference entity, where the spread input itself has to be estimated rather than observed.
Q6 — A Level 1 position has no uncertainty to catch: the market price IS the value, verifiable by checking a second independent quote source in seconds

Every position technically gets SOME check, but the depth and cost of that check scales with how much judgment went into the original number. Section 07's takeaway makes this proportionality explicit: Level 3 positions draw more scrutiny "because there's no independent market quote to check a Level 1 mark against a second source; there's only a Level 3 mark's model and assumptions to check" — the checking mechanism itself has to be fundamentally different (re-run the model with independently-sourced assumptions), not just more thorough in the same way.

Q7 — Two related but genuinely different problems: IFRS 13 cares about balance-sheet transparency; BCBS FRTB cares about how much regulatory capital a bank must hold

Section 08 states this directly: FRTB "explicitly penalizes banks... for holding positions with less-observable, harder-to-verify valuations" — the SAME Level 1/2/3 observability concept IFRS 13 uses for balance-sheet transparency gets reused by BCBS for an entirely different purpose (how much loss-absorbing capital a bank needs to hold), which is exactly why Section 08's takeaway flags this module as the first to sit at the intersection of trading and financial-reporting disciplines rather than belonging cleanly to just one.
