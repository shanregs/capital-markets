01 · What is Capital Markets?

Primary vs Secondary Market

Capital Markets is where entities raise money by issuing securities, and where investors then trade those securities among themselves. Almost everything you'll see in trade data is the second thing.

ActorRole
Government of CanadaIssuer — creates the bond, raises cash
Initial InvestorsFirst owners of the new bond at issuance
Goldman SachsInvestor reselling the bond later (secondary seller)
Bank Of TorontoInvestor / dealer buying in the secondary market

Primary Market — new issuance

Flow · Primary IssuanceISIN CA1358XXXXX
Government of Canada                         Investors

       |                                            |
       |  issues "Canada 5% Bond 2035"               |
       |  Total Face Value: CAD 500,000,000          |
       |-------------------------------------------->|
       |     (brand-new bonds, ISIN: CA1358XXXXX)     |
       |                                            |
       |<--------------------------------------------|
       |        CAD 500,000,000 cash                  |
       |                                            |
Result: Government raises CAD 500,000,000 cash. The bond "Canada 5% Bond 2035" now exists for the first time.

Coupon Rate vs. Yield — what does "5%" actually mean?

The 5% in "Canada 5% Bond 2035" is called the coupon rate, not APR (APR is loan/credit terminology). The coupon rate is fixed at issuance and never changes — it's a label printed on the bond. What does change over time is the yield: the effective annualized return an investor actually earns, which depends on the price they paid, not just the coupon.

Concept · Coupon vs. YieldThe "true APR"
Coupon Rate  = fixed, printed on the bond, never changes
               "Canada 5% Bond 2035" always pays 5% of face value/year

Yield        = the REAL annualized return, given what you actually paid
               - Buy at par (100.00)   -> Yield ~= Coupon Rate (5.00%)
               - Buy below par (98.50) -> Yield  >  Coupon Rate (you paid
                                           less, still get 5% + your
                                           money back at par -> better return)
               - Buy above par (101.80)-> Yield  <  Coupon Rate

Yield is the number that behaves like "APR" — the honest, price-adjusted effective annual return. Coupon rate is closer to a label on the product. Full yield math (Yield to Maturity) is covered properly in Module 4/8 — for now, just don't conflate the two.

Worked example — Bank Of Toronto buys a different 5%-coupon bond each year

The clearest way to see "coupon rate never changes, yield always does" is to watch the same 5% coupon behave differently every year, purely because the price paid and the face value bought are different each time.

Formulas · Coupon vs. YieldFixed vs. Derived
Annual Coupon Cash Flow = Face Value x Coupon Rate       <- FIXED, issuer decides
Current Yield            = Annual Coupon Cash Flow / Price Paid   <- VARIES, market decides

Price Paid = Face Value x (Market Price / 100)
YearFace Value (CAD)CouponMarket PricePrice PaidCoupon Cash FlowCurrent YieldYield vs. CouponBank Of Toronto's Position
202610,000,0005%98.50 (discount)9,850,000500,0005.08%Yield > CouponMore profit — paid below face value, so the fixed coupon is a bigger return on cash actually put down.
202715,000,0005%101.25 (premium)15,187,500750,0004.94%Yield < CouponLess profit — paid above face value; same coupon, smaller return on cash put down.
20288,000,0005%95.00 (deep discount)7,600,000400,0005.26%Yield > CouponBest year of the four — biggest discount to par, biggest gap in Bank Of Toronto's favor.
202920,000,0005%103.75 (premium)20,750,0001,000,0004.82%Yield < CouponWorst year of the four — largest premium paid, real return furthest below the printed rate.
  • Coupon Rate (5%) — identical in every row; a static field on the security master, fixed at issuance, never recalculated.
  • Price — a market-driven field, refreshed daily; the only reason the same 5% coupon produces four different outcomes above.
  • Yield — always a derived field: Coupon Cash Flow ÷ Price Paid. If a system stores Yield as static, it's already wrong the moment Price moves.
Reading "is Bank Of Toronto better or worse off" off the coupon rate alone is meaningless — 2028 and 2029 both show a 5% coupon, yet one is Bank Of Toronto's best year and the other its worst, purely from price paid. This uses simple Current Yield for clarity; Yield to Maturity (YTM) — which also folds in the gain/loss recovered when price unwinds back to par at maturity — is covered properly in Module 4/8.
One Issuance, Many Investors — can multiple investors buy slices of the same bond?

Yes — this is the normal case, not the exception. The total CAD 500,000,000 face value is one big pool that gets split across many investors, each taking whatever face-value slice they want (in minimum denominations, e.g. CAD 1,000 or CAD 5,000 lots). Every slice shares the exact same ISIN, coupon rate (5%), and maturity (2035) — they're fungible units of one security, the same way every share of Apple stock is identical regardless of who holds how many.

This is exactly why the secondary market example below works: Goldman Sachs, holding a CAD 120,000,000 slice from issuance, can later sell just CAD 10,000,000 of it to Bank Of Toronto — every unit of face value of that ISIN is identical and interchangeable. Only the quantity held differs between investors.

How allocation actually happens (briefly): government bonds are typically issued via an auction — investors submit bids for how much face value they want and at what price/yield, and the government allocates the total across accepted bids. Corporate bonds usually go through syndication/book-building — underwriting banks canvas demand from many buy-side investors and allocate the issue accordingly. Either way: one ISIN, one coupon, many holders.

Why 98.50? — bond price mechanics explained

Why divide by 100 — it's a quoting convention, not math you derive

Bond prices are quoted as "price per 100 of face value" — a rate, not a dollar amount.

Convention · Price to ValueRate × Scale
Quoted Price = 98.50  means:
"For every CAD 100 of face value, this bond costs CAD 98.50 today."

To get the cash value of ANY face amount traded, scale by that ratio:

  Market Value = Face Value Traded x (Quoted Price / 100)

Example: Face Value Traded = CAD 10,000,000
  Market Value = 10,000,000 x (98.50 / 100)
               = 10,000,000 x 0.9850
               = CAD 9,850,000

Why "per 100" instead of a flat dollar figure? Because the same bond trades in wildly different face-value chunks — Goldman Sachs might trade CAD 10,000,000 face value, another investor CAD 250,000 face value, of the exact same bond. One universal "per 100" rate lets everyone use the same quoted number regardless of trade size — exactly like an FX exchange rate (EUR/USD = 1.08) or a unit price multiplied by quantity.

Why is the price 98.50 and not 100?

At issuance, a bond is usually priced at (or near) 100 — called "par." You pay CAD 100 and get back CAD 100 at maturity, plus coupons. After issuance, bond prices move inversely to prevailing market interest rates:

Mechanic · Price vs. Interest RatesInverse relationship
At issuance:
  Canada 5% Bond 2035 issued at Price = 100.00 (par)
  Market interest rates at the time  ~= 5.00%
  -> A 5% coupon is exactly competitive. Fair price = 100.

Later, market interest rates rise to ~5.30%:
  -> Newly issued bonds now offer 5.30%, better than this old bond's 5%.
  -> Nobody pays 100 for a bond paying 5% when 5.30% is available.
  -> The bond's PRICE must drop so its effective yield rises to compete.
  -> Market repricing settles around Price = 98.50.

(If rates had instead FALLEN to ~4.70%, this bond's fixed 5% would look
 attractive, and its price would rise ABOVE par, e.g. 101.80.)
Not an arbitrary discount — 98.50 is the market's real-time answer to "given today's interest rates, what does this fixed 5% cash-flow stream have to cost so a buyer earns a fair, competitive return?" Full mechanics (yield, duration) are covered in Module 4/8. For now: price moves opposite to interest rates.
Is the seller (Goldman Sachs) actually taking a loss?

You cannot tell from 98.50 alone — it depends on what the seller originally paid, plus coupons collected along the way.

Scenario · Cost Basis vs. CouponsNet P&L
Scenario: Goldman Sachs bought this bond 2 years ago AT ISSUANCE (par = 100.00)
          Face value = CAD 10,000,000
          Cost then  = CAD 10,000,000  (10,000,000 x 100.00/100)

Today, Goldman Sachs sells at 98.50:
          Proceeds now = CAD 9,850,000  (10,000,000 x 98.50/100)

Price-only comparison:
          9,850,000 - 10,000,000 = -CAD 150,000   <- looks like a loss

BUT Goldman Sachs also collected 2 years of coupons while holding it:
          Coupon = 5% x CAD 10,000,000 = CAD 500,000 / year
          2 years of coupons = CAD 1,000,000

Net result over the holding period:
          -150,000 (price)  +  1,000,000 (coupons)  =  +CAD 850,000 net gain

  -> Even though the SALE PRICE is below what they originally paid,
     Goldman Sachs still came out ahead once coupon income is included.

Two more nuances worth keeping: the 98.50 price says nothing about either party's individual P&L — it's just today's fair market price, and Bank Of Toronto isn't "getting a discount" by buying at it. And a seller might sell below their original cost for reasons unrelated to "loss" — needing cash, rebalancing a portfolio, or expecting rates to rise further. Selling "below par" is routine, not inherently a bad decision.

Mental model: Price (98.50) = today's fair value per 100 face value, driven by interest rates. Gain/loss = today's price vs. your own cost basis, plus coupons collected. Two completely different questions.
Takeaway. If you see "Trade" data in a reporting system, assume secondary market unless the record explicitly says "New Issue" / "Primary" / "Syndication".

03 · Who owns the risk

Dealer vs Broker

The core question for any actor: does this party take the risk onto its own book, or just pass it through?

Dealer (Principal) — Bank Of Toronto takes the bond onto its own book

Flow · Principal TradeHeld on book
Client A                    Bank Of Toronto                Client B
(Seller)                    (DEALER)                       (Buyer)
   |                              |                              |
   | Sells CAD 5,000,000 bond     |                              |
   | @ price 99.00                |                              |
   |------------------------------>|                              |
   |<------------------------------|                              |
   |     Cash CAD 4,950,000        |                              |
   |                              |                              |
   |                        [ Bond now sits on Bank Of Toronto's
   |                          OWN balance sheet — Bank Of Toronto
   |                          carries MARKET RISK until resold ]
   |                              |                              |
   |                              |  Later, sells to Client B     |
   |                              |  @ price 99.20                |
   |                              |------------------------------>|
   |                              |<------------------------------|
   |                              |     Cash CAD 4,960,000        |
Bank Of Toronto's profit = 4,960,000 − 4,950,000 = CAD 10,000 (and it carried price risk on the bond in between the two trades).

Broker (Agent) — Bank Of Toronto never owns the bond

Flow · Agency TradePass-through
Client A                    Bank Of Toronto                Client B
(Seller)                    (BROKER)                       (Buyer)
   |                              |                              |
   |--[order: sell CAD 5M bond]-->|                              |
   |                              |--[matches order]------------->|
   |                              |                              |
   |<===============  Bond, CAD 5,000,000  ===================>|
   |<===============  Cash, CAD 4,950,000  ====================|
   |      (bond & cash move DIRECTLY between the two clients;    |
   |       Bank Of Toronto's books are never touched)             |
   |                              |                              |
   |------[pays commission CAD 2,000]------------------------------>|
   |                              |<------------------------------|
   |                              |    Commission CAD 2,000       |
Bank Of Toronto's profit = CAD 2,000 commission only. Zero market risk held on the bond itself.

Fund

A fund (e.g., Maple Leaf Pension Fund above) pools many individuals' or institutions' money and invests it according to a mandate. It is almost always a buy-side actor sitting on the client side of dealer/broker trades like the ones above.

Takeaway. "Never holding the bond itself" is the tell for a broker. A dealer, by definition, does hold it — even briefly — and that's exactly what creates its market risk.

06 · When cash and securities actually move

Settlement Conventions — T, T+1, T+2 with real dates

T ("Trade Date") is the day the trade is agreed. T+n ("Settlement Date") is n business days later — weekends and market holidays don't count, which is why T+2 isn't just "add 2 to the calendar date."

ProductStandard settlement
US & Canadian equitiesT+1 (moved from T+2 in May 2024)
Government / corporate bondsT+1 or T+2, depending on market
FX spotT+2 for most pairs (T+1 for USD/CAD)
OTC derivatives (e.g. IR Swaps)No notional settles — only periodic net cash flows (Section 08)

Example 1 — no weekend in the way

Calendar · Clean WeekT+2
Trade Date (T)  : Monday,    17 August 2026
T+1             : Tuesday,   18 August 2026
T+2             : Wednesday, 19 August 2026   <- Settlement Date

Example 2 — a weekend sits in the middle

Calendar · Weekend CrossedT+2
Trade Date (T)  : Thursday, 20 August 2026
T+1             : Friday,   21 August 2026
                   Saturday, 22 August 2026  <- not a business day, skipped
                   Sunday,   23 August 2026  <- not a business day, skipped
T+2             : Monday,   24 August 2026   <- Settlement Date

Example 3 — a public holiday also sits in the way

Calendar · Holiday CrossedT+2
Trade Date (T)  : Thursday,  3 September 2026
T+1             : Friday,    4 September 2026
                   Saturday,  5 September 2026  <- weekend, skipped
                   Sunday,    6 September 2026  <- weekend, skipped
                   Monday,    7 September 2026  <- Labour Day (holiday), skipped
T+2             : Tuesday,   8 September 2026    <- Settlement Date
T+2 landed 5 calendar days after T, not 2 — because only business days count, and this stretch had both a weekend AND a holiday.
Takeaway. If a "Settlement Date" field is more than 2 calendar days after "Trade Date," that's not automatically a data bug — check whether a weekend or market holiday fell in between before assuming something's broken.

07 · Who physically holds/moves the securities

Custodian vs CSD

An investor never holds an account directly at the central ledger — there's always a custodian in between.

Custodian

A bank that holds securities in safekeeping on behalf of a client (an investor or dealer), carries out settlement instructions, collects coupon/dividend payments on the client's behalf, and produces account statements. Example real-world custodians: BNY Mellon, State Street, Northern Trust.

CSD (Central Securities Depository)

The single, central electronic ledger for a given market that records the ultimate legal ownership of securities in book-entry form (no paper certificates) — the place where final transfer of ownership actually happens on Settlement Date. Each market typically has one main CSD: CDS (Canada), DTC (US), Euroclear / Clearstream (international/Eurobonds).

Flow · Custody ChainSettlement Date, T+2
Bank Of Toronto  ---(client of)--->  Custodian (e.g. BNY Mellon)
                                            |
                                  (custodian holds an account at)
                                            v
                                  CSD (e.g. CDS / DTC)  <- master ledger
                                            ^
                                  (custodian holds an account at)
                                            |
Goldman Sachs    ---(client of)--->  Custodian (its own, or same one)

On Settlement Date (T+2):
  1. Both custodians instruct the CSD to move the bond, on their
     clients' behalf, from Goldman Sachs' custody account to
     Bank Of Toronto's custody account.
  2. The CSD updates its central book-entry record — this is the
     LEGAL moment ownership changes.
  3. Cash moves separately via the banking payment system, ideally
     at the same time (Delivery vs Payment - DvP) so neither side
     is left holding one leg without the other.
Takeaway. "Custodian" = your own bank holding your positions and dealing with settlement on your behalf. "CSD" = the one central record-of-truth ledger for a market that every custodian ultimately plugs into. When reconciling positions (Back Office, Section 04), your internal books should tie out to your custodian's statement — which itself ties out to the CSD.

08 · What actually settles on a derivative

Notional Amount & a first look at Interest Rate Swaps

Notional amount is the reference principal used only to calculate periodic interest cash flows on a derivative — it is never physically exchanged. That's the key difference from a bond trade: a bond's face value is actually delivered (Section 01); a swap's notional is just a number used for arithmetic.

Revisiting the OTC swap from Section 05 — Bank Of Toronto and Goldman Sachs, Notional CAD 100,000,000, 5-year maturity, quarterly payments, Bank Of Toronto pays a fixed rate, Goldman Sachs pays a floating rate (CORRA + spread):

Worked Example · One Quarterly PaymentNotional CAD 100,000,000
Notional (reference only, NEVER exchanged): CAD 100,000,000
Period: ~91 days (one quarter), day count actual/365

Bank Of Toronto's FIXED leg:
  100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91 / 365) = CAD 997,260

Goldman Sachs' FLOATING leg (CORRA averaged 3.20% + 0.05% spread = 3.25%):
  100,000,000 x 3.25% x (91 / 365) = CAD 810,068

NETTING  (only the difference actually moves, one direction):
  997,260 - 810,068 = CAD 187,192
Result: Bank Of Toronto pays Goldman Sachs CAD 187,192 net on this date. The CAD 100,000,000 notional itself never moves — on this date or any other — it only ever exists to compute the two legs above. This repeats every quarter for 5 years, with the floating leg reset each period to whatever CORRA + spread then is.
This is deliberately just the mechanics of one cash flow, not the theory of swaps — pricing/valuing a swap (discount curves, DV01/PV01, mark-to-market) is covered properly in Module 7: FX & Interest Rate Products. For now, keep two things: (1) notional is a calculation input, not a cash amount that settles, and (2) in practice only one net payment moves per period, not two gross ones.
Takeaway. If you see a derivative trade record with a huge "Notional" value (e.g. CAD 100,000,000) and no corresponding settlement of that amount anywhere in cash/custody systems, that's expected — notional isn't supposed to settle. Only the periodic net cash flows do.

Q3 — Settlement Date = Wednesday

Monday (T) → Tuesday (T+1) → Wednesday (T+2) — no weekend falls in between since all three days are weekdays.

  • Front Office is active on T (Monday) — executes the trade.
  • Middle Office is active on T, same day (Monday) — risk checks, valuation, confirmation/affirmation.
  • Back Office is active on T+2 (Wednesday) — delivers bonds, receives cash, books ledger entries, reconciles, reports.
Q4 — The exchange itself is the public record; OTC has no such record

An Apple/NASDAQ trade doesn't need separate Trade Repository reporting because the exchange is already a public, regulated, central venue — regulators see every trade through the exchange's own data feed in real time. The CAD 100,000,000 IR Swap is OTC: a private, bilateral contract between exactly two parties, with no central venue recording it. Regulators would have zero visibility into it unless both counterparties are legally required to separately report the trade to a Trade Repository (e.g. under EMIR / MiFID II) — which is exactly the requirement that exists.

Q6 — A custodian is client-facing; a CSD is the one central ledger the whole market shares

A custodian is the bank that holds an investor's or dealer's securities in safekeeping, executes settlement instructions on their behalf, and produces statements — many different custodians exist, each serving their own roster of clients. A CSD is the single central ledger for a market that records the ultimate legal ownership of every security issued in that market. An investor's custodian needs its own CSD account because the CSD doesn't deal directly with every individual investor — it only maintains a manageable number of large participant accounts (custodians, clearing members, major banks), each of which aggregates and nets the positions of many underlying clients beneath it. The investor's true legal ownership is only as good as this whole chain reconciling correctly, end to end.
