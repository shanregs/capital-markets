01 · A loan, not an ownership claim

What Is Fixed Income? — Theory & Terminology

A Fixed Income instrument is, at its core, a loan. The buyer lends money to the issuer; the issuer promises to pay periodic interest (the coupon) and return the principal (Face Value) at a fixed future date (Maturity). This is the mirror image of Module 4's Equity: a bond is a creditor claim, not an ownership claim.

TermMeaning
Face ValueThe principal amount repaid at maturity; also the base the coupon is calculated against
CouponThe fixed periodic interest payment, expressed as a % of Face Value (Module 1 §1)
MaturityThe fixed date the Face Value is repaid — the defining feature equity doesn't have
PriceQuoted per 100 of Face Value (Module 1 §1)
YieldThe buyer's actual return given the price paid, not just the coupon rate (Module 1 §1)

Coupon Rate vs. Yield was already covered in full, with a four-year worked example, in Module 1 §1 — the short version: Coupon Rate is fixed and printed on the bond; Yield is derived and moves with Price. That distinction is assumed from here on.

Clean Price vs Dirty Price vs Accrued Interest — new territory

Bond prices are always quoted as Clean Price — no interest included. But a buyer settling between coupon dates owes the seller Accrued Interest — the coupon the seller "earned" since the last coupon date. The actual cash exchanged at settlement is the Dirty Price (aka "Full Price"/"Invoice Price") — Clean Price plus Accrued Interest.

Math · Clean vs Dirty PriceCanada 5% Bond 2035
Bond: Canada 5% Bond 2035, semi-annual coupon (2.50% per period)
Face Value: CAD 10,000,000
Quoted (Clean) Price: 98.50
Settlement falls 60 days after the last coupon date, in a
182-day coupon period

Accrued Interest = Face Value x (Coupon/2) x (Days Since Last Coupon / Days in Period)
                  = 10,000,000 x 2.50% x (60 / 182)
                  = 250,000 x 0.32967
                  = CAD 82,418  (rounded)

Clean Price Value = 10,000,000 x 98.50 / 100 = CAD 9,850,000
Dirty Price (actual cash paid) = 9,850,000 + 82,418 = CAD 9,932,418

-> The seller held the bond for 60 of the 182 days in this
   coupon period, so 60/182 of the next coupon belongs to them,
   even though the BUYER will be the one who actually receives
   the full coupon payment on the next coupon date.
IT/reporting angle: most systems store a bond's "Price" field as the Clean Price — the market convention, what moves on a price feed. But the cash that actually settles (Module 1 §07) is always the Dirty Price. If a settlement amount doesn't match Quantity × Clean Price / 100, check whether Accrued Interest was added and whether the trade fell between coupon dates before assuming a bug.

Duration — a one-line preview

Modified Duration approximates how much a bond's price moves for a 1% change in yield. A bond with Duration 8 sees its price fall roughly 8% if yields rise 1%. The full math (and DV01) belongs to Module 10 (Valuation) — for now: longer maturity and lower coupon generally mean higher duration, meaning more price sensitivity to rate moves.

Takeaway. Fixed Income terminology all serves one goal — separating what's fixed at issuance (Face Value, Coupon Rate, Maturity) from what's market-driven and changes daily (Price, Yield, Accrued Interest, and therefore Dirty Price). Getting this split right is the single most useful mental model for any bond-related reporting field.

02 · Who's borrowing the money

What Are The Products? — Government, Corporate, Municipal & Other Debt

The further down this list you go, the more the instrument's risk profile is driven by who the issuer is.

Diagram · Fixed Income Product TreeBy issuer type

├── Corporate Bonds         (issued by companies, incl. banks like Bank Of Toronto)
├── Municipal Bonds         (issued by cities/provinces/states/municipalities)
└── Other Debt Instruments  (money market & securitized: T-Bills, CP, CDs, MBS/ABS/CLO)
ProductWhat it isExample
Government BondsDebt issued by a national government; typically the benchmark risk-free rate in its own currencyCanada 5% Bond 2035
Corporate BondsDebt issued by a company; carries credit risk government debt doesn'tBank Of Toronto 4.5% Bond 2030

A US-specific nuance worth flagging: interest on many US municipal bonds is exempt from US federal income tax (and often state tax for a resident of the issuing state) — a unique feature without a broad Canadian equivalent, and a major reason US munis trade at lower yields than similarly-rated corporate bonds.

Takeaway. A Government Bond's main variable is interest-rate risk (Module 8: Rates); a Corporate Bond adds credit risk (Module 9: Credit) on top of the same coupon/price/yield mechanics.

03 · The bond itself vs. a financing contract on it

What Exactly Is Being Traded? — Security vs Contract

A bond itself is an actual security. Fixed Income introduces a product Equity doesn't have an equivalent of yet: repo — a contract, not a security, built on top of a bond.

Diagram · Bond vs RepoSecurity vs Financing Contract
Canada 5% Bond 2035 (SECURITY):
  A real, transferable claim on the Government of Canada. It exists
  independently of any one trade, the same way Coca-Cola stock does
  (Module 4 §3).

Repo — "Repurchase Agreement" (CONTRACT):
  Bank Of Toronto lends EUR 50,000,000 cash overnight to Goldman
  Sachs, who posts Germany 3% Bund 2033 as COLLATERAL and agrees
  to repurchase it (i.e., return the cash + interest and get the
  Bund back) the next business day.

  Overnight interest = 50,000,000 x 3.15% x (1/365) = EUR 4,315
  (this is the exact trade from systems-landscape §12's SFTR
  worked example — a repo IS a Securities Financing Transaction)

-> The Bund itself doesn't change hands as a SALE — legal title
   temporarily transfers as COLLATERAL, with a contractual promise
   to reverse the transfer the next day. This is economically a
   secured cash loan, not a purchase of the bond.
Takeaway. This is Fixed Income's version of Module 4 §03's "security vs contract" distinction — but where an Equity Option is a derivative referencing a stock's price, a Repo is a financing contract using a bond as collateral. Different category of contract entirely, which is exactly why it's reported under SFTR (Section 08) rather than EMIR.
Schema note: Repo has no .avsc schema at all in the work data model taxonomy — it isn't a capital-markets "Trade" product the way a Bond or an IRS is, it's a Securities Financing Transaction, modeled (and reported, per SFTR) through an entirely separate SFT data pipeline outside this taxonomy's scope.

4c. Municipal Bond

Municipal Bond · City of Toronto Green Bond3.75% 2032
Issuer: City of Toronto (proceeds earmarked for green
        infrastructure — a "use of proceeds" bond)
Buyer:  Maple Leaf Pension Fund
Dealer: Bank Of Toronto

Trade: CAD 15,000,000 face value @ Price 97.25
Trade Value = 15,000,000 x 97.25 / 100 = CAD 14,587,500

Note: unlike a US municipal bond, this Canadian municipal bond's
interest is fully taxable to Maple Leaf Pension Fund — the
broad tax-exemption feature (Section 02) is specifically a US
municipal-bond feature, not a general "municipal bond" one.
This is a primary issuance, not a secondary-market trade. City of Toronto's bond doesn't exist yet before this trade — Bank Of Toronto is underwriting/placing a brand-new bond (Module 1 §1's primary-market mechanics), not intermediating a security that's already outstanding between two other market participants the way a secondary-market dealer trade would.
Diagram · Primary Issuance Chain — Who Owes What to WhomCity of Toronto → Bank Of Toronto → Maple Leaf Pension Fund
Maple Leaf Pension Fund  ---- pays CAD 14,587,500 ---->  Bank Of Toronto

                               fee/spread) --------------> City of Toronto

-> Bank Of Toronto is the underwriter/placement agent standing
   between a NEW issuer and the end buyer — a different role
   than Section 4a's Government Bond trade, where Bank Of
   Toronto is simply the BUYER of an already-existing security
   with no issuer in the chain at all.
Schema: BondTrade.avsc — same schema again; the Dealer role (Bank Of Toronto) is captured as a party reference on the trade, not a different schema.

4d. Other Debt Instrument — Treasury Bill

Treasury Bill · Government of Canada 90-DayDiscount instrument
Buyer: Bank Of Toronto (money-market desk)

Face Value: CAD 20,000,000, Maturity: 90 days, NO coupon
Purchase Price: 98.75 (per 100) -- bought at a DISCOUNT to par

Purchase Cost = 20,000,000 x 98.75 / 100 = CAD 19,750,000
Discount Earned = 20,000,000 - 19,750,000 = CAD 250,000
                  (this IS the return -- there is no coupon)

Discount Yield = (Discount / Purchase Cost) x (365 / Days to Maturity)
               = (250,000 / 19,750,000) x (365 / 90)
               = 1.2658% x 4.0556
               = 5.13%

-> T-Bills return purely the gap between discounted purchase
   price and full Face Value at maturity -- there's no coupon
   to separate from yield the way Section 01 did for the 5% bond.
Schema: BondTrade.avsc — still the same schema; a zero-coupon discount instrument is a Coupon Rate of 0% on the same schema, not a different product type in the taxonomy.
Takeaway. All four examples share the exact same core fields (Face Value, Price, Trade Value) from Module 2 §03 — what changes is who the issuer is, whether a coupon exists at all (the T-Bill has none), and the tax treatment. The mechanics of "how a Fixed Income trade is priced and valued" are consistent across the whole asset class; only the credit and tax profile shifts.

Order → Execution → Trade Capture → Confirmation → Clearing → Settlement → Position → Valuation/P&L → Reporting, applied to the Government Bond trade from Section 04a, executed in Canada.

Takeaway. The single biggest lifecycle difference from Module 4's Equity walkthrough is that Fixed Income's Valuation/P&L stage splits into two components every single day — price movement and interest accrual — while an equity position only has one (price movement; dividends are episodic, not continuously accruing). A bond P&L report that only shows price movement is silently dropping the accrual leg.

07 · The opposite default from Equity

Exchange vs OTC vs Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared

Unlike Module 4's Equity, where "exchange-traded" and "centrally cleared" mostly travel together, cash Fixed Income trades are overwhelmingly OTC and bilateral. Repo is the interesting exception.

BilateralCentrally Cleared
Exchange-Tradedn/a — bonds essentially never trade on an exchangeA few retail bond platforms exist, but volume is tiny
OTCMOST Government, Corporate, and Municipal bond trades — dealer-to-dealer or dealer-to-client, no CCPRepo, increasingly: CDCC (Canada, since 2012), FICC (US Treasury repo)
ProductExchange or OTC?Bilateral or Centrally Cleared?
Government BondOTCBilateral (the norm)
Corporate BondOTCBilateral (the norm)
Municipal BondOTCBilateral (the norm)
RepoOTCEither — bilateral by default, increasingly routed through CDCC/FICC for the largest, most standardized activity
Takeaway. This is the opposite default from Module 4's Equity table, where bilateral-and-exchange-traded was "effectively never" and centrally-cleared was the norm. For Fixed Income, bilateral OTC is the norm and central clearing is the (growing) exception — exactly why bond market regulation (Section 08) leans so heavily on transaction reporting (MiFIR) rather than relying on a CCP's own records.

08 · Two bodies that matter more here than in Equity

Regulatory Perspective — MiFID, EMIR, SFTR, FCA, CIRO, HKMA, DTCC, DMO

Two regulators flagged as tangential in Module 4 turn out to be squarely on-topic here.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Fixed Income specifically
MiFID II / MiFIR (EU)Bond transaction reporting and pre/post-trade transparency — with different thresholds than equities (e.g. "large-in-scale" waivers reflecting how much less liquid bonds are)
EMIRDoes not apply to a cash bond trade. Would apply to a bond future/option (Module 8: Rates) — the Fixed Income equivalent of Module 4 §08's equity-derivatives carve-out
SFTRApplies directly to repo (Section 03) — this is the module where the systems-landscape §12 example's underlying instrument, Germany 3% Bund 2033, actually comes from
FCA (UK)Conduct regulator for UK bond trading, administers UK MiFIR bond transaction reporting
CIRO (Canada)SRO overseeing Canadian bond dealer conduct
HKMA (Hong Kong)Genuinely more central here than for Equity (Module 4 §08): the HKMA directly issues Exchange Fund Bills & Notes and acts much like a debt management office for Hong Kong, in addition to its central-bank role
DTCC (US)Its subsidiary FICC specifically clears US Treasuries and Treasury repo — the Fixed Income-specific piece Module 4 didn't need to reach for
DMO (UK Debt Management Office)This is its module: the DMO issues UK Gilts via auction — the UK's counterpart to how the Government of Canada issues bonds (Module 1 §1) and how the US Treasury issues Treasuries. See the UK DMO Reporting Deep Dive for exactly what a GEMM reports back to the DMO
Takeaway. HKMA and DMO turn out to be squarely on-topic here — a reminder that the regulatory perspective genuinely depends on the asset class in front of you, exactly as the eight-part pattern intends: the same acronym can be the main actor in one module and a footnote in another.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Accrued Interest = CAD 164,835; Dirty Price = CAD 10,014,835
Math · 120 Days AccruedCanada 5% Bond 2035
Accrued Interest = 10,000,000 x 2.50% x (120 / 182) = 250,000 x 0.65934 = CAD 164,835
Dirty Price = 9,850,000 (Clean) + 164,835 = CAD 10,014,835

The later into the coupon period settlement falls, the more accrued interest has built up (approaching the full CAD 250,000 semi-annual coupon as days-since-last-coupon approaches 182) — exactly the linear accrual mechanic Section 01 established.

Q2 — A T-Bill pays no coupon at all; its entire return is the discount

Section 01's coupon-vs-yield distinction only exists because a coupon bond has two numbers that can diverge (a fixed coupon rate and a price-dependent yield). A T-Bill has only one return mechanism — the discount — so "yield" is the only number that exists; there's nothing for it to be compared against.

Q3 — Goldman Sachs is neither cleanly a buyer nor a seller — economically, it's a cash borrower posting the Bund as collateral

Section 03 states this directly: legal title to the Bund temporarily transfers, but the whole transaction is structured as a secured loan with a contractual promise to reverse the transfer — which is precisely why repo is classified as a Securities Financing Transaction (SFTR, Section 08) rather than an outright purchase/sale that MiFID transaction reporting would otherwise capture.

Q4 — It most likely indicates the trade is actually a bond derivative (Module 8: Rates), not a cash bond — or it's a data-quality error

Section 06's takeaway states this explicitly: UPI is for OTC derivatives; a cash bond should never carry one. Seeing it populated on an otherwise bond-shaped record is the same diagnostic signal Module 2 §02 established for Tickers on bonds, just running the check in the opposite direction.

Q5 — CDCC (Canada, since 2012) and FICC (US Treasury repo)

Repo is the exception because, unlike outright government/corporate/municipal bond trades (which stay bilateral OTC), it's increasingly routed through a CCP for standardized activity. Section 07's table lists both by name, tying directly back to systems-landscape §04 (CDCC) and §05 (FICC).

Q6 — It most likely means the instrument isn't actually a plain cash bond — it's a bond future or bond option (Module 8: Rates)

Section 08's table states EMIR does not apply to a cash bond, only to a derivative — seeing EMIR fields populated is the strongest possible signal that whatever "Fixed Income-looking" trade this is, it's actually a derivative in disguise, and belongs in the Rates asset class instead.

Q7 — The HKMA directly issues Exchange Fund Bills & Notes, Hong Kong's own government debt
In Module 4 (Equity), HKMA was flagged as a step removed from the action, with the SFC doing the real work of equity conduct regulation. Here, HKMA is directly the issuer-side actor for Hong Kong government debt, the same functional role the DMO plays for UK Gilts (Section 08) — a concrete illustration that the same regulator's relevance shifts with the asset class.
Q8 — No to both — the Section 04a/05 trade settles via CDSX only, touching neither CDCC nor CSA MI 96-101

It's a plain cash Government of Canada bond purchase, so it touches neither CDCC (repo-specific) nor CSA MI 96-101 (OTC-derivative-specific). If the SAME bond were instead posted as collateral in a repo, the back-office path changes to CDCC's repo-clearing service, but CSA MI 96-101 STILL wouldn't apply — Canada has no dedicated SFT reporting regime as of 2026, unlike the EU/UK's SFTR. Section 09's worked diagram states this explicitly: the same underlying bond produces two different back-office paths (CDSX for an outright purchase, CDCC for repo) depending purely on which contract type it's wrapped in — Section 03's security-vs-contract distinction, now traced all the way through to which Canadian system actually touches the trade.
