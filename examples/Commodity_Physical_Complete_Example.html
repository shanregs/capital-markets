01

What is a Physical Commodity Trade?

A physical commodity trade is the purchase or sale of the actual, tangible good — gold, crude oil, wheat — with real delivery, storage, and title transfer. Like a cash equity or bond, this is not a contract that derives its value from something else; the commodity itself IS the thing being traded.

Physical Commodity ↔ Real, deliverable tangible good

03

Why Physical Commodities Rarely Sit on a Bank's Own Book

Physical Gold
   |
   +-- Storage cost (vaulting, insurance)
   +-- Delivery logistics
   +-- No coupon, no dividend — pure price exposure
   +-- Illiquid to unwind quickly

This is exactly why Module 7 §2 notes that physical commodities are "rare in a bank's own book — usually accessed via derivatives instead" (see the Commodity Swap example). A bank wanting price exposure to gold, without the storage headache, would use a Commodity Swap or Future instead.

Settlement — Physical Delivery, Not DvP via a CSD

Unlike a security, there is no CSD for a gold bar. Settlement instead means:

Seller's vault                             Buyer's vault
     |                                          |
     |  100 oz gold bar (physical transfer)     |
     |----------------------------------------->|
     |<---------------------------- USD 265,000 |
     |                                          |
   Bilateral, dealer-to-dealer, verified by assay/serial number

08

Daily Valuation

Suppose the gold spot price moves to USD 2,710.00/oz one month later:

Market Value = Quantity × Spot Price
             = 100 × 2,710.00
             = USD 271,000

Unrealized P&L = 271,000 − 265,000 = +USD 6,000

Module 10 §2 classifies this as Quote-Based valuation — same simple family as a cash equity or bond.

09

Contango and Backwardation — Why This Matters Even for a Spot Holding

If Bank Of Toronto wanted to know what a future delivery of gold should cost (relevant if it's weighing physical storage against a Commodity Future instead), it would compare today's spot price to the futures curve:

Futures Price = Spot Price + Storage Cost + Financing Cost − Convenience Yield

If the futures curve slopes upward (later delivery costs more), the market is in Contango; if it slopes downward, it's in Backwardation (Module 7 §1). This concept doesn't affect the physical trade's settlement mechanics — it only matters when comparing physical ownership to a derivative alternative.

11

Regulatory Perspective

Physical commodity trades sit mostly outside derivative reporting regimes:

RegulationApplies?
EMIR / Dodd-FrankNo — not a derivative
MiFID II / MiFIRGenerally no, for pure physically-settled spot commodity trades used commercially
SFTRNo — not a financing transaction

Compare this to the Commodity Swap example, which is squarely inside EMIR's scope.

13

The Three Most Important Physical Commodity Concepts

1. It's a real, deliverable good

Unlike a swap's notional, the 100 oz genuinely changes vaults.

2. Storage and logistics are real costs

Total cost of ownership = Purchase Price + Storage + Insurance + Financing

3. Valuation is just Quantity × Spot Price

Market Value = Quantity × Spot Price

14

Mental Model Recap

Desire for real commodity ownership → Physical Trade → Real delivery, real storage, real price exposure

Physical Commodity sits at the "Cash" end of the commodity product family, exactly parallel to how Cash Equity sits at the "Cash" end of the equity product family — everything else in Module 7 (Future, Option, Swap) is a derivative built on top of this same underlying good.
