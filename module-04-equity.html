01 · Fractional ownership, not a loan

What Is Equity? — Theory & Terminology

Equity represents fractional ownership in a company. A shareholder owns a proportional claim on the company's assets and future earnings, evidenced by shares. Unlike the Fixed Income instruments in Module 5, equity has no maturity date, no fixed coupon, and no promise to return principal — the return comes entirely from price appreciation and dividends, both variable and, for dividends, discretionary.

TermMeaning
ShareOne unit of ownership in a company
Outstanding SharesTotal number of shares currently held by all shareholders
Market CapitalizationShare Price × Outstanding Shares — the market's valuation of the whole company
DividendA discretionary cash (or stock) payment to shareholders, declared by the board — never guaranteed
Voting RightsCommon shareholders typically get one vote per share on corporate matters
Corporate ActionAn issuer-initiated event that changes the share (stock split, buyback, spin-off, rights issue)

Equity vs Fixed Income — the contrast that matters most

EquityFixed Income (Module 5)
Ownership claimYes — a residual claim on the companyNo — a creditor claim (a loan to the issuer)
MaturityNone — held indefinitelyFixed — bond matures on a set date
ReturnPrice appreciation + dividends (variable)Coupon (fixed, contractual) + principal at maturity
Priority in bankruptcyLast — paid only after all creditorsAhead of equity — bondholders are creditors
Takeaway. Every other section in this module — products, trade lifecycle, identifiers, regulation — traces back to this one fact: a share is a piece of the company itself, not a promise from it. That's the opposite of a bond, and it's why equity valuation (Module 10) looks completely different from bond valuation.

02 · The security vs. contracts on the security

What Are The Products? — Cash Equity vs Equity Derivatives

Two buckets: the real security, and contracts whose value derives from it.

Diagram · Equity Product TreeCash vs Derivatives
EQUITY
│
├── Cash Equity              (the actual security)
│   ├── Common Stock
│   └── Preferred Stock
│
└── Equity Derivatives       (contracts whose value derives from the security)
    ├── Equity Futures
    └── Equity Options
ProductWhat it is
Common StockOrdinary shares — one vote per share (typically), dividends at the board's full discretion, last claim on assets if the company is wound up
Preferred StockA hybrid instrument — carries a fixed dividend rate like a bond coupon, but the dividend is still only paid if declared; ranks ahead of common stock but behind all debt in bankruptcy; usually no voting rights
Equity FutureAn exchange-traded contract to buy/sell a stock or index at a future date at an agreed price; index futures are typically cash-settled
Equity OptionA contract giving the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) shares at a fixed strike, by/at expiry, for an upfront premium
Takeaway. Common and Preferred Stock sit in the same "Cash Equity" bucket because both are real, deliverable securities — the difference is entirely in the economics of the claim. Futures and Options sit in a different bucket entirely, because neither one is the stock — Section 03 makes this distinction concrete.

03 · The core distinction

What Exactly Is Being Traded? — Security vs Contract

For Common or Preferred Stock, you're trading an actual security — a real, transferable ownership claim recorded in book-entry form at a CSD (Module 1 §07). For Equity Futures or Options, you're trading a contract whose value derives from that security's price — created at execution, typically extinguished at expiry or exercise.

Diagram · Security vs ContractCoca-Cola stock vs AAPL option
Common Stock (SECURITY):
  Coca-Cola Company issued a fixed number of shares, years ago.
  Every trade just transfers ownership of EXISTING shares between
  two parties -- nothing new is created or destroyed by trading it.

Equity Option (CONTRACT):
  "AAPL 15-Jan-2027 250 Call" doesn't exist until two parties agree
  to create it (the same principle as Module 1 §8's notional, applied
  to options) -- and it disappears entirely at expiry/exercise.
  Trading it doesn't move any Apple shares; it creates or transfers
  a CLAIM on Apple shares.
Takeaway. This is the "Quantity vs Notional" distinction from Module 2 §03, restated for equity specifically. A cash equity trade's Quantity (shares) is a real, deliverable amount. An equity derivative's underlying share count is closer to a reference amount used to size the contract's payoff — whether it ever results in real shares moving depends entirely on whether the contract is physically settled and actually exercised (Section 05).

04 · Buyer → Seller → Trade

Real-World Trade Examples

Four trades, four different actor relationships — a bilateral dealer trade, a primary issuance, an exchange trade against a CCP, and a bilateral option written by a dealer.

4a. Common Stock

Common Stock · Coca-Cola CompanyNYSE: KO
Buyer:  Maple Leaf Pension Fund
Seller: Goldman Sachs
Dealer: Bank Of Toronto (takes the trade onto its own book briefly,
        per Module 1 §3's dealer/principal mechanics)

Trade: 10,000 shares @ USD 68.40
Trade Value = 10,000 x 68.40 = USD 684,000
Schema: EquityTrade.avsc — Trade only. There's no EquityValuation.avsc in the work data model taxonomy: cash equity is marked using a shared/generic security-pricing model (Section 05's daily mark-to-market just needs a market price, not product-specific valuation logic the way an option or swap needs).
Diagram · One "Trade," Two LegsWhat "Dealer" Actually Means Here
Leg 1:  Goldman Sachs  ---- sells 10,000 KO @ 68.40 ---->  Bank Of Toronto
Leg 2:  Bank Of Toronto ---- sells 10,000 KO @ 68.40 ---->  Maple Leaf Pension Fund

Both legs settle at the SAME price -- Bank Of Toronto takes no
spread and ends the day flat (bought 10,000, sold 10,000) -- but
it is a REAL counterparty on each leg, not a pass-through broker.
Bank Of Toronto is a genuine counterparty to both sides here — a principal, per Module 1 §3's dealer/principal mechanics — which means the single "Trade:" line above is really two separate settlement obligations: Bank Of Toronto owes Goldman Sachs cash and owes Maple Leaf Pension Fund shares, and separately is owed shares from Goldman Sachs and cash from Maple Leaf Pension Fund. If Bank Of Toronto were instead acting as a riskless broker (never taking title), there would be only one obligation, directly between Goldman Sachs and Maple Leaf Pension Fund — that is NOT what's happening in this example.

4b. Preferred Stock

Preferred Stock · Series ABank Of Toronto issuance
Issuer: Bank Of Toronto (primary issuance, per Module 1 §1's
        primary-market mechanics)
Buyer:  Goldman Sachs

Issue: 2,000,000 shares @ Par CAD 25.00, Dividend Rate 4.50%
       Non-Cumulative
Cash Raised = 2,000,000 x 25.00 = CAD 50,000,000

Annual Dividend / share = 25.00 x 4.50% = CAD 1.125
Total Annual Dividend    = 2,000,000 x 1.125 = CAD 2,250,000
Paid Quarterly           = 2,250,000 / 4 = CAD 562,500 / quarter

"Non-Cumulative" means: if Bank Of Toronto's board skips a
quarter's dividend, Goldman Sachs does NOT get it later -- it is
simply gone. Contrast a bond coupon (Module 1 §1), where missing
a payment is a DEFAULT. A preferred dividend, even on a fixed-rate
instrument, is never a legal obligation the way a bond coupon is.
Schema: EquityTrade.avsc — same schema as Common Stock. The taxonomy doesn't split Common vs. Preferred; both are "Cash Equity" and share one Trade schema (Section 02), with the fixed-vs-discretionary dividend distinction living in reference/economic-terms data, not a different schema.

4c. Equity Future

Equity Future · E-mini S&P 500CME
Buyer: Maple Leaf Pension Fund
Seller/Counterparty: CME Clearing (the CCP — see Section 07; on an
      exchange, buyer and seller are matched anonymously and each
      ends up facing the CCP, not each other, from day one)

Trade: BUY 20 contracts @ 5,920.00 (index points)
       Multiplier: USD 50 per index point
Notional = 20 x 50 x 5,920.00 = USD 5,920,000

  20 x 12,000 = USD 240,000 posted to CME Clearing

  Variation Margin = (5,935.00 - 5,920.00) x 50 x 20 = USD 15,000

Schema: FuturesTrade.avsc / FuturesValuation.avsc — not an equity-specific schema. In the work data model taxonomy, all exchange-traded futures (equity index, rate, commodity, FX — any underlying) sit under the cross-cutting "7. Listed Derivatives" node (Module 15), not under "4. Equity." This equity index future is taught here for the underlying concept, but its actual schema mapping lives in Module 15.

4d. Equity Option

Equity Option · AAPL Call15-Jan-2027 250 Call
Buyer: Maple Leaf Pension Fund
Seller (writer): Bank Of Toronto (dealer)

Trade: BUY 100 contracts, Strike USD 250, Expiry 15-Jan-2027
       Premium: USD 18.50 / share, 100 shares / contract
Premium Paid = 100 x 100 x 18.50 = USD 185,000 (paid T+1)

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario A: AAPL trades at USD 270
  Intrinsic Value = (270 - 250) x 10,000 shares = USD 200,000
  Option exercised; Net Profit = 200,000 - 185,000 = USD 15,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario B: AAPL trades at USD 240
  Option expires WORTHLESS (strike above market price)
  Maple Leaf Pension Fund loses the full USD 185,000 premium
  -> this is the maximum possible loss for a long option buyer,
     ever — never more than the premium paid.
Schema: ListedOptionsTrade.avsc / ListedOptionValuation.avsc — not EquityOptionTrade.avsc. This AAPL call is exchange-listed and clears via the OCC (Section 05), so in the taxonomy it belongs to the cross-cutting "7. Listed Derivatives" node (Module 15) alongside Futures, same as Section 04c. EquityOptionTrade.avsc / EquityOptionValuation.avsc is reserved for a bilateral OTC equity option — same economic product, different schema, and the deciding factor is Exchange vs. OTC (Section 07), not "is it an equity option."
Same clearing relationship as Section 04c's future: once this option clears via the OCC, Maple Leaf Pension Fund and Bank Of Toronto each actually face the OCC, not each other — the "Buyer"/"Seller (writer)" labels above describe the trade's original two-sided economics, not who ends up counterparty-exposed to whom after novation.
Takeaway. Notice the buyer/seller relationship changes shape across these four examples — a bilateral dealer trade (4a), a primary issuance (4b), an exchange trade against a CCP (4c), and a bilateral option written by a dealer (4d). All four are still "Equity," but the actors and the settlement mechanics already look different before Section 05 even gets to lifecycle.

05 · Nine stages, applied to equity

Trade Lifecycle — Equity-Specific Walkthrough

Order → Execution → Trade Capture → Confirmation → Clearing → Settlement → Position → Valuation/P&L → Reporting — the same backbone as Module 3, with two stages (Position and Valuation/P&L) made explicit.

Full Lifecycle · Coca-Cola Common StockBOT-EQ-2026-011203
09:31:00  ORDER — Maple Leaf Pension Fund instructs Bank Of Toronto:
          "BUY 10,000 shares Coca-Cola Company (NYSE: KO)"

09:31:04  EXECUTION @ USD 68.40 on NYSE

09:31:04  TRADE CAPTURE — Trade ID BOT-EQ-2026-011203

09:45:00  CONFIRMATION/MATCHING via DTCC CTM -> MATCHED

Trade submitted to NSCC for CLEARING (multilateral netting,
per systems-landscape §05 — this trade may net against other
KO trades Bank Of Toronto did that day)

Settlement Date = T+1
          DTC performs DvP: shares move in DTC's book-entry
          system; cash settles via Fedwire.

POSITION — Maple Leaf Pension Fund's holding of KO increases by
          10,000 shares in the position-keeping system (this is
          what Module 3 §08's reconciliation checks against the
          custodian and CSD records)

VALUATION / P&L — each day after settlement, the position is
          marked to the current market price:
            Day of purchase:  10,000 x 68.40 = USD 684,000 (cost)
            One week later:   10,000 x 71.10 = USD 711,000 (market value)
            Unrealized P&L =  711,000 - 684,000 = +USD 27,000

REPORTING — trade reported to the relevant Trade Repository/
          regulator by T+1 (Module 3 §09); the ongoing POSITION
          is separately subject to periodic position reporting
          requirements in some jurisdictions (Module 14)

How Clearing differs for the derivative products

ProductCCPWhat "Clearing" adds
Common/Preferred StockNSCC (US example above) / CDCC / LCH / Eurex, per countryMultilateral netting before one T+1/T+2 DvP settlement
Equity FutureCME ClearingNovation (Module 3 §06) plus daily Variation Margin for the life of the contract
Listed Equity OptionOCC (Options Clearing Corporation)Novation of the option; premium settles T+1; if exercised, a separate physical stock settlement follows T+1 after exercise
A naming collision worth flagging: "OCC" means two completely different things in US capital markets. Here, in an equities/derivatives context, it's the Options Clearing Corporation — the CCP for all US-listed equity options. In a banking/prudential context (systems-landscape §10), "OCC" instead means the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency — the regulator that charters and supervises national banks. Same acronym, same country, unrelated organizations — always check context.
Takeaway. The "Position" and "Valuation/P&L" stages aren't new concepts — they're Module 3 §08 (Reconciliation) and a preview of Module 10 (Valuation), made explicit here because equity is where they're easiest to see: a stock position just sits there being marked to a public, constantly-moving market price, with no coupon or notional math to obscure it.

FIGI's distinguishing feature versus ISIN is that it can identify a security at more than one level — a "Composite" FIGI for the security globally, and separate FIGIs per exchange listing (useful for a dual-listed stock, where ISIN alone doesn't tell you which venue's price/liquidity a given trade record refers to).

07 · Two separate questions

Exchange vs OTC vs Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared

"Exchange vs OTC" asks where the trade was negotiated (Module 1 §05). "Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared" asks what the counterparty risk looks like after execution (Module 3 §06) — even an OTC trade can end up centrally cleared if later novated to a CCP.

BilateralCentrally Cleared
Exchange-TradedEffectively never for equity — exchange rules typically require clearingCommon/Preferred Stock, Equity Futures (CME Clearing), Listed Options (OCC)
OTCUncleared equity swaps/options between two dealers, subject to bilateral collateral (Uncleared Margin Rules, Module 2 §07)A growing share of equity index swaps now clear via a CCP (LCH, Eurex) even though negotiated OTC
Equity productExchange or OTC?Bilateral or Centrally Cleared?
Common/Preferred StockExchange-tradedCentrally cleared (NSCC/CDCC/LCH/Eurex per country)
Equity FutureExchange-tradedCentrally cleared (CME Clearing)
Listed Equity OptionExchange-tradedCentrally cleared (OCC)

Takeaway. For cash equity and listed derivatives, "exchange-traded" and "centrally cleared" travel together so consistently that people often treat them as synonyms — but Section 08's regulatory reporting obligations (EMIR, SFTR) exist specifically because the bottom-right cell of the first table (bilateral OTC) is where a trade can otherwise go completely unseen by a regulator, exactly as Module 1 §05 first established for OTC products generally.

08 · Acronyms attached to something real

Regulatory Perspective — MiFID, EMIR, SFTR, FCA, CIRO, HKMA, DTCC, DMO

Only now, with the products, trade mechanics, and identifiers already concrete, do the acronyms get attached to something real.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Equity specifically
MiFID II / MiFIR (EU)Pre/post-trade transparency for equity trading, best-execution obligations, the Systematic Internalizer regime, T+1 transaction reporting (Module 3 §09, systems-landscape §07)
EMIRApplies only if the equity derivative is OTC (e.g. a TRS on KO) — reported to a Trade Repository (Module 2 §07). Does not apply to Common/Preferred Stock or listed Futures/Options
SFTRApplies when equity shares are lent/borrowed, or used as repo collateral — e.g. Bank Of Toronto lending its KO position to a short-seller (systems-landscape §12)
FCA (UK)Conduct regulator for UK equity trading — administers UK MiFIR equity transaction reporting (systems-landscape §10)
CIRO (Canada)SRO overseeing Canadian equity dealer conduct and trading activity (systems-landscape §10)
HKMA / SFC (Hong Kong)Worth a careful distinction: HKMA is Hong Kong's central-bank-equivalent (banking supervision, monetary policy) — analogous to the Bank of England. The SFC (Securities and Futures Commission) is Hong Kong's actual securities/equities conduct regulator — the closer analog to the FCA or SEC
DTCC (US)Its subsidiaries NSCC and DTC clear and settle US cash equities (Section 05; systems-landscape §05)
DMO (UK Debt Management Office)Issues UK Gilts — not an equity-relevant body; full treatment belongs in Module 5 (Fixed Income)
Takeaway. Every regulation above attaches to a specific mechanic already covered in Sections 01–07 — MiFID to where/how the trade executed, EMIR to whether the derivative is OTC, SFTR to whether shares themselves (not just a derivative on them) were lent or used as collateral. None of these rules apply uniformly to "Equity" as a whole; each is scoped to a particular slice of what this module just walked through.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Goldman Sachs receives CAD 0 for the two skipped quarters; if cumulative, CAD 1,687,500 total

Section 04b defines this directly: "non-cumulative" means a skipped dividend is simply gone, unlike a cumulative preferred share (or a bond coupon, which would constitute default if missed) where unpaid amounts accrue and must eventually be paid before any dividend can resume to common shareholders. If cumulative: 2 x CAD 562,500 = CAD 1,125,000 in skipped amounts, plus the regular CAD 562,500 for the third quarter = CAD 1,687,500 total catch-up.

Q2 — Variation Margin = -USD 20,000, moving FROM Maple Leaf Pension Fund's margin account
Math · Variation Margin, Down Move5,920.00 → 5,900.00
Variation Margin = (5,900.00 - 5,920.00) x 50 x 20 = -USD 20,000

Since Maple Leaf Pension Fund is long (bought) the future and the index fell, the position lost value — the daily mark-to-market mechanic from Section 04c runs the same way regardless of direction, it just moves money the other way when price moves against the position holder.

Q3 — Maximum possible loss = USD 185,000, the full premium paid

Fixed at trade date because it's simply the price paid upfront; the maximum possible gain is theoretically unlimited (or very large) because it depends on how high AAPL's price can rise before expiry, which isn't knowable at trade date. Section 04d's Scenario B shows this exact outcome — the option expiring worthless caps the loss at premium paid, no matter how far below the strike AAPL falls. This asymmetry (fixed, known maximum loss vs. open-ended potential gain) is the defining economic feature of being long an option, contrasted with Section 04c's future, where both gains and losses are theoretically unbounded in either direction.

Q4 — Two entirely separate CCPs, not the same organization

Section 05's table and the disambiguation callout make this explicit: NSCC (a DTCC subsidiary) clears cash equity trades; OCC (Options Clearing Corporation) is a completely different, independent CCP dedicated specifically to US-listed options. They happen to both ultimately settle the underlying shares through DTC when an option is exercised, but the clearing function itself sits in two different organizations.

Q5 — Bank Of Toronto is identified by its LEI; the instrument by ISIN, CUSIP, and Ticker

Section 06's diagram states this directly — LEI answers "who," while ISIN/CUSIP/Ticker (and, where relevant, SEDOL/FIGI) all answer "what." Maple Leaf Pension Fund and Goldman Sachs would each carry their own separate LEIs as buyer/seller on the same trade record.

Q6 — No, it stays OTC. Central clearing only changes the Bilateral-vs-Centrally-Cleared axis

Section 07's two-axis model draws this distinction precisely: "where the trade was negotiated" (OTC, since it was a bilateral dealer negotiation) is permanently fixed by how the trade originated, regardless of what happens to it afterward. Submitting it for clearing only moves it from the "OTC + Bilateral" cell to the "OTC + Centrally Cleared" cell — it never becomes "Exchange-Traded," because it was never executed on an exchange.

Q7 — SFTR governs it
Lending the actual shares (not a derivative referencing them) is a Securities Financing Transaction, exactly the product family SFTR exists for (systems-landscape §12). MiFID doesn't apply because this isn't a MiFID-scope "executed transaction" on the shares — no sale/purchase happened. EMIR doesn't apply because a securities lending transaction is not an OTC derivative — there's no notional-referencing contract here, just the shares themselves changing hands temporarily. The key distinguishing test, consistent with Module 2's asset-class hierarchy: is this a trade in the security itself, or a contract referencing the security? Securities lending is squarely the former, and specifically the financing sub-category SFTR was built for.
Q8 — No, it does NOT go to a Trade Repository. CIRO's UMIR-based market surveillance and CDSX's settlement process (with Bank of Canada FMI oversight) capture and oversee it instead

Section 09's table states this directly: TR reporting is an OTC-derivatives-specific mechanism (Module 2 §07, Module 14 §1), not a cash-equity one. The Loblaw trade is instead captured by (1) CIRO's UMIR-based market surveillance, ingesting the execution data from TSX (or whichever venue it traded on) for conduct/surveillance purposes, and (2) CDSX's Continuous Net Settlement process at CDS, which nets and settles the trade DvP, T+1 — with the Bank of Canada providing systemic oversight of CDSX itself as a designated FMI.
