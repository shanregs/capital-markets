## 1. The Hierarchy — from Asset Class down to a single Trade

**Definition:** Every record you'll ever see in a reporting system sits at the bottom of a four-layer hierarchy. The top three layers describe **what** is being traded and never change. The bottom layer describes **one specific event** and gets a brand-new record every single time.

```text
ASSET CLASS  ->  PRODUCT  ->  INSTRUMENT  ->  TRADE
========================================================

Asset Class:  Fixed Income
                    |
Product:       Government Bond
                    |
Instrument:    "Canada 5% Bond 2035"    (ISIN: CA1358XXXXX)
                    |
Trade:         Bank Of Toronto buys CAD 10,000,000 face value
               from Goldman Sachs @ Price 98.50, Trade Date 17-Aug-2026
```

| Layer | What it answers | Example value | Changes per trade? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Asset Class** | "What broad category of thing is this?" | Fixed Income | No — fixed for the instrument's entire life |
| **Product** | "What kind of Fixed Income product?" | Government Bond | No |
| **Instrument / Security** | "Which exact tradable thing?" | Canada 5% Bond 2035 (ISIN CA1358XXXXX) | No — one instrument, referenced by thousands of trades |
| **Trade** | "Who bought/sold how much, at what price, when?" | Bank Of Toronto buys CAD 10,000,000 @ 98.50 on 17-Aug-2026 | **Yes** — a brand-new record every time |

**Takeaway:** Asset Class, Product, and Instrument are reference data — look them up once, they don't move. Trade is transactional data — there can be thousands of Trade records all pointing at the exact same Instrument (exactly like the "One Issuance, Many Investors" example in [Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md)).

## 2. Instrument Identifiers — ISIN vs CUSIP vs Ticker

**Definitions:**

- **ISIN** (International Securities Identification Number) — 12 characters, globally unique: 2-letter country code + a 9-character national security identifier + 1 check digit. The de-facto global standard identifier for a security, used across bonds, equities, and increasingly derivatives.
- **CUSIP** (Committee on Uniform Securities Identification Procedures) — 9 characters, North America only (US & Canada). For North American securities, the CUSIP is literally embedded inside the ISIN.
- **Ticker** (trading symbol) — a short, human-readable code used on a specific exchange (e.g. `AAPL`). It is **not** globally unique — the same ticker can mean different things on different exchanges — and it's really only meaningful for exchange-traded instruments (mainly equities). Bonds and OTC derivatives don't have tickers.

```text
IDENTIFYING THE SAME KIND OF THING, THREE DIFFERENT WAYS
=============================================================

Apple Inc. common stock (equity, exchange-traded on NASDAQ):
  Ticker : AAPL
  CUSIP  : 037833100
  ISIN   : US 037833100 5
           ^^ US (country) + 037833100 (CUSIP, embedded) + 5 (check digit)

Canada 5% Bond 2035 (government bond — NOT exchange-traded):
  Ticker : (none — bonds don't trade under exchange tickers)
  CUSIP  : 135087XX1   (illustrative)
  ISIN   : CA 135087XX1 X
           ^^ CA (country) + CUSIP-style body + check digit
```

| Identifier | Scope | Typical asset classes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ISIN** | Global — one per security, worldwide | All: equities, bonds, some derivatives (via UPI) | `US0378331005` |
| **CUSIP** | North America only | Equities & bonds issued/traded in US/Canada | `037833100` |
| **Ticker** | Exchange-specific, not globally unique | Mainly exchange-traded equities | `AAPL` |

**Takeaway:** if a reporting record has only a Ticker and no ISIN/CUSIP, that's a strong signal it's equity data straight from an exchange feed — not a bond or OTC derivative, which will almost always carry an ISIN instead.

## 3. Core Trade Fields — Trade ID, Quantity vs Notional, Price, Currency

**Definitions:**

- **Trade ID** — a unique reference generated by the executing firm's trading system the instant a trade is captured. It's the thread that ties one specific transaction together across its entire lifecycle ([Module 3](./module-03-trade-lifecycle.md)).
- **Quantity** — for equities and bonds, the actual amount of the instrument bought or sold (shares, or face value). It physically moves at settlement.
- **Notional** — for derivatives, a reference principal used only to calculate periodic cash flows. It never physically moves (full treatment in [Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md), Section 8).
- **Price** — what one unit costs, in whatever convention that instrument uses: per share, per 100 face value, or an FX rate.
- **Currency** — the currency the trade is denominated and settled in.

### Worked example — Bank Of Toronto's blotter, three asset classes, one day

```text
BANK OF TORONTO'S BLOTTER — 17 AUGUST 2026
==============================================

TRADE 1 — BOND
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000512
  Instrument : Canada 5% Bond 2035  (ISIN CA1358XXXXX)
  Quantity   : CAD 10,000,000 face value
  Price      : 98.50  (per 100 face value)
  Currency   : CAD
  Value      : 10,000,000 x 98.50 / 100 = CAD 9,850,000

TRADE 2 — EQUITY
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000513
  Instrument : Apple Inc. common stock  (Ticker AAPL, ISIN US0378331005)
  Quantity   : 5,000 shares
  Price      : USD 227.15 per share
  Currency   : USD
  Value      : 5,000 x 227.15 = USD 1,135,750

TRADE 3 — OTC DERIVATIVE
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000514
  Instrument : 5Y CAD Interest Rate Swap vs Goldman Sachs
  Notional   : CAD 100,000,000   (reference only — NEVER exchanged)
  Price      : Bank Of Toronto pays Fixed 4.00% / receives Floating
  Currency   : CAD
  Value      : no upfront value exchanged — only periodic net cash flows
```

**Takeaway:** "Quantity" and "Notional" are not interchangeable, even though some systems reuse the same database column for both. Quantity moves — bonds and shares actually change hands at settlement. Notional never moves — only the cash flows calculated from it do.

## 4. One Hierarchy, Three Asset Classes — side by side

The same four-layer mental model applies everywhere, but what actually settles at the bottom of it is different enough per asset class to justify a dedicated module later ([Module 4](./module-04-equity.md) for bonds, [Module 5](./module-05-fixed-income.md) for equity, [Module 6](./module-06-fx.md)/7 for derivatives).

| Asset Class | Product | Instrument (example) | Identifier | "Size" field | What actually settles |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed Income | Government Bond | Canada 5% Bond 2035 | ISIN | Quantity (face value) | Face value + accrued interest |
| Equity | Common Stock | Apple Inc. shares | Ticker / ISIN | Quantity (shares) | Shares + cash |
| Derivatives (OTC) | Interest Rate Swap | 5Y CAD IRS | No ISIN typically (UPI under newer rules) | Notional (reference only) | Only net cash-flow differences |

**Takeaway:** if you can name the Asset Class, Product, and Instrument, you can already guess what "settlement" will mean for that trade before ever opening [Module 4](./module-04-equity.md)/5/6 — bonds and equities move something real, OTC derivatives (mostly) don't.

## 5. Full Field Reference — what each field on a trade record actually means

Using Trade 1 (the bond) from Section 3 as the running example:

| Field | Meaning | Example value | Static (reference) or new per trade? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset Class | Broad category | Fixed Income | Static |
| Product | Category within the asset class | Government Bond | Static |
| Instrument / Security | The specific tradable thing | Canada 5% Bond 2035 | Static |
| ISIN | Global identifier for the instrument | CA1358XXXXX | Static |
| CUSIP | North American identifier for the instrument | 135087XX1 | Static |
| Ticker | Exchange trading symbol (mainly equities) | n/a for this bond | Static |
| Trade ID | Unique reference for this one transaction | BOT-2026-000512 | **New per trade** |
| Notional | Reference principal (derivatives only) | n/a for this bond | **New per trade**, where applicable |
| Quantity | Actual amount transacted | CAD 10,000,000 face value | **New per trade** |
| Price | Cost per unit | 98.50 | **New per trade** |
| Currency | Denomination / settlement currency | CAD | Usually static per instrument, but confirmed on every trade |

**Takeaway:** when debugging a reporting field, the first question is "is this a static reference-data field (Asset Class → Instrument) or a per-trade transactional field (Trade ID → Currency)?" — that answer alone tells you whether to look at the security master or the trade capture system.

## 6. Important Asset Classes & Products — Cash Instruments (not ISDA-classified)

**Definition:** "Cash" (or "physical") instruments are securities that exist and settle on their own — a bond or a share is a real, deliverable thing the moment it's issued. These are classified using **ISIN/CUSIP** (which instrument) plus a **CFI code** (ISO 10962 — *Classification of Financial Instruments*, a 6-character code baked into most security masters that says, e.g., "this is a debt instrument, straight bond, fixed rate, registered form"). Cash instruments do **not** get an ISDA classification — ISDA taxonomy is specifically for derivatives (Section 7).

| Asset Class | Key Products | Example Instrument | Typical Identifier | What "size" means | What actually settles |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed Income — Government** | Treasury Bills, Government Bonds, Sovereign Bonds | Canada 5% Bond 2035 | ISIN | Face value (Quantity) | Face value + accrued interest |
| **Fixed Income — Corporate** | Investment-Grade Bonds, High-Yield Bonds, Convertible Bonds | Bank Of Toronto 4.5% Bond 2030 | ISIN | Face value (Quantity) | Face value + accrued interest |
| **Fixed Income — Securitized** | MBS (Mortgage-Backed), ABS (Asset-Backed), CLO (Collateralized Loan Obligation) | Fannie Mae 30Y MBS Pool #12345 | ISIN / CUSIP | Face value (Quantity), pays down over time | Face value (amortizing) + interest |
| **Money Market** | T-Bills, Commercial Paper, Certificates of Deposit, Repo | 90-Day Commercial Paper — Bank Of Toronto | ISIN / CUSIP | Face value, short maturity (<1yr) | Face value at maturity (usually issued at discount) |
| **Equity — Common** | Common Stock, ADR/GDR (cross-listed shares) | Apple Inc. common stock | Ticker / ISIN | Number of shares (Quantity) | Shares + cash |
| **Equity — Preferred/Fund** | Preferred Stock, ETFs, Mutual Fund Units | S&P 500 ETF units | Ticker / ISIN | Number of units (Quantity) | Units + cash |
| **FX — Spot/Cash** | FX Spot | USD/CAD spot | Currency pair (no ISIN) | Notional currency amount | Both currencies, T+1/T+2 ([Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md) §6) |
| **Commodity — Physical** | Physical Gold, Physical Oil (rare in a bank's own book — usually accessed via derivatives instead) | 100oz Gold Bar | No ISIN — commodity code | Physical unit (oz, barrel) | Physical delivery (rare) or cash-settled equivalent |

**Takeaway:** every row above is something you could, in principle, physically hold — a bond certificate (book-entry today, but still "a thing"), a share, a bar of gold. That's exactly why none of them carry an ISDA classification: ISDA only classifies *contracts between two parties*, which is what a derivative is (Section 7) — not the underlying tradable thing itself.

## 7. What Is ISDA, and Why Does a Derivative Need an "ISDA Classification"?

**Definition:** **ISDA** (International Swaps and Derivatives Association) is the trade body that standardizes two separate things people often conflate:

1. **The ISDA Master Agreement** — the legal contract template two counterparties sign once, which then governs *every* OTC derivative trade between them (netting, default, collateral terms). Covered properly in [Module 12](./module-12-collateral-margin.md) (Collateral & Margin).
2. **The ISDA Product Taxonomy** — a standardized *classification scheme* for labelling what kind of derivative a trade is. This is the piece relevant to [Module 2](./module-02-hierarchy.md) and to regulatory reporting ([Module 14](./module-14-regulatory-reporting.md)): when Bank Of Toronto reports an OTC derivative trade to a Trade Repository under EMIR, MiFIR, or Dodd-Frank/CFTC rules, one of the mandatory fields on that report is literally "ISDA Taxonomy" (or a field derived from it) — it's how the regulator's systems bucket millions of trades from every firm into comparable categories without reading each contract by hand.

The ISDA Product Taxonomy is a **four-level hierarchy** — notice it's a different hierarchy from Section 1's Asset Class → Product → Instrument → Trade; this one classifies the *product* itself for regulatory purposes, one level more granular than our "Product" layer:

```text
ISDA PRODUCT TAXONOMY  —  four levels
=========================================

  ISDA Asset Class   ->   Base Product   ->   Sub-Product   ->   Transaction Type
  ==================================================================================

  Interest Rate      ->   IRSwap         ->   FixedFloat     ->   Swap
  Credit             ->   SingleName     ->   Corporate      ->   CreditDefaultSwap
  Foreign Exchange   ->   Forward        ->   Deliverable    ->   Forward
  Equity             ->   Swap           ->   PriceReturn    ->   Swap
  Commodity          ->   Energy         ->   Oil            ->   Swap
```

There are exactly **five official ISDA Asset Classes** for derivatives — a much shorter, fixed list compared to the open-ended "Asset Class" values you'd see across cash + derivative products combined:

| ISDA Asset Class | Plain-English meaning | Common desk name |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest Rate** | Derivatives whose cash flows depend on interest rates | "Rates" |
| **Credit** | Derivatives whose payout depends on a default/credit event | "Credit" |
| **Foreign Exchange** | Derivatives whose payout depends on currency rates | "FX" |
| **Equity** | Derivatives whose payout depends on a stock/index price | "Equity Derivatives" |
| **Commodity** | Derivatives whose payout depends on a physical commodity price | "Commodities" |

**Takeaway:** if a reporting record has an "ISDA Taxonomy" or "ISDA Asset Class" field populated, that alone tells you it's an OTC derivative, not a cash bond or cash equity — cash instruments simply don't have this field, because ISDA doesn't classify them (Section 6).

## 8. ISDA Derivative Asset Classes & Products — Master Table

This is the derivatives counterpart to Section 6's cash-instrument table — same idea, but every row here is a **contract**, not a deliverable thing, so notice the "Notional" and "What actually settles" columns look completely different from Section 6.

| ISDA Asset Class | Key Products (Base Product) | Example Instrument | Sub-Product / Transaction Type | What actually settles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Interest Rate** | Interest Rate Swap (IRS), Forward Rate Agreement (FRA), Swaption, Cap/Floor, Cross-Currency Swap | 5Y CAD IRS, Bank Of Toronto pays fixed / receives floating | FixedFloat / Swap | Only periodic net cash flows ([Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md) §8) |
| **Credit** | Single-Name CDS, CDS Index (e.g. CDX, iTraxx), Total Return Swap (TRS) | 5Y CDS on "Northern Rail Corp", Bank Of Toronto buys protection | SingleName-Corporate / CreditDefaultSwap | Periodic premium; payout only if a credit event occurs (Section 9) |
| **Foreign Exchange** | FX Forward, FX Swap, FX Option, Non-Deliverable Forward (NDF) | 3M USD/CAD Forward, Bank Of Toronto buys USD | Forward-Deliverable / Forward | Both currency amounts on value date (or net cash for NDFs) (Section 10) |
| **Equity** | Equity Option, Equity Swap, Equity Index Future, Variance Swap | Total Return Swap on Apple Inc. shares | Swap-PriceReturn / Swap | Periodic net cash flows tracking the share's price/dividends |
| **Commodity** | Commodity Swap, Commodity Forward, Commodity Option | 1Y Brent Crude Oil Swap, notional 100,000 barrels | Energy-Oil / Swap | Periodic net cash flows referencing a commodity price index (almost never physical barrels) |

**Takeaway:** across all five ISDA asset classes, the pattern from [Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md) §8 repeats — a Notional/reference amount sits on the trade purely to calculate cash flows, and only the netted difference (or, for Credit, a contingent payout) actually moves. Physical settlement of the underlying (e.g. real oil barrels) is the rare exception, not the norm, for OTC derivatives.

## 9. Worked Example — Credit Default Swap (CDS)

**Definition:** A CDS is insurance-like protection against a company defaulting. The **protection buyer** pays a periodic premium (the "spread," quoted in basis points per year) to the **protection seller**. If the **reference entity** (the company being insured against) suffers a defined **credit event** (default, bankruptcy, restructuring), the protection seller pays the buyer a loss amount — otherwise, the seller collects premium and pays nothing.

**A CDS has only two parties — the reference entity isn't one of them.** Northern Rail Corp is the *subject* of this contract, not a *party* to it: it signs nothing, pays nothing, receives nothing, and typically has no idea the trade exists. The link is purely referential — its credit behavior is the trigger condition the two real counterparties' cash flows depend on, the same way an insurance policy on someone else's house pays out based on that house, without the homeowner being a party to the policy.

```text
THREE NAMES, ONLY TWO PARTIES TO THE CONTRACT
========================================================================

  Bank Of Toronto  &lt;====  CDS CONTRACT  ====&gt;  Goldman Sachs
  (protection buyer)     (bilateral,            (protection seller)
                          just these two)

                    Northern Rail Corp
                 (reference entity -- outside the
                  contract, referenced BY it, not
                  a party TO it)

  -&gt; Bank Of Toronto pays Goldman Sachs premium; Goldman Sachs pays
     Bank Of Toronto a loss amount IF Northern Rail Corp defaults.
     Northern Rail Corp itself pays or receives NOTHING under this
     contract, ever.
```

In practice, a protection buyer usually has *some other* exposure to the reference entity it's hedging — e.g. Bank Of Toronto might hold Northern Rail Corp bonds or have lent it money directly — and uses the CDS to transfer that separate credit risk to Goldman Sachs. Nothing about the CDS contract itself requires that exposure to exist, though; a CDS can equally be bought purely to speculate on Northern Rail Corp's credit quality with no underlying position at all.

```text
CREDIT DEFAULT SWAP  —  Bank Of Toronto buys protection from Goldman Sachs
================================================================================

  Reference Entity : Northern Rail Corp (a corporate borrower — fictional)
  Notional          : CAD 20,000,000  (reference only — never exchanged upfront)
  Spread            : 120 bps (1.20% per year)
  Maturity          : 5 years, quarterly premium payments
  Recovery Rate     : 40% (assumed, if default occurs)

SCENARIO A — No default this quarter (the normal case)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Quarterly premium (Bank Of Toronto pays Goldman Sachs):
    20,000,000 x 1.20% x (91 / 365) = CAD 59,836

  Bank Of Toronto  ---- pays CAD 59,836 ---->  Goldman Sachs
  (repeats every quarter for 5 years = 20 payments, unless default happens first)

SCENARIO B — Northern Rail Corp (the reference entity) defaults during the 5 years
----------------------------------------------------------
  Loss Given Default = Notional x (1 - Recovery Rate)
                      = 20,000,000 x (1 - 0.40)
                      = CAD 12,000,000

  Goldman Sachs  ---- pays CAD 12,000,000 ---->  Bank Of Toronto
  (premium payments then stop — the contract has been triggered)
```

**ISDA classification for this trade:** Asset Class = **Credit**, Base Product = **SingleName**, Sub-Product = **Corporate**, Transaction Type = **CreditDefaultSwap**. This is exactly the four-level label a Trade Repository report for this trade would carry.

**Takeaway:** notice the CAD 20,000,000 notional never appears in either cash flow above — it's purely the multiplier for the premium calculation, and the base for the loss-given-default calculation. Whether Bank Of Toronto ever sees CAD 12,000,000 depends entirely on whether Northern Rail Corp defaults; most CDS contracts run their full term paying only the small quarterly premium and never trigger.

## 10. Worked Example — FX Forward

**Definition:** An FX Forward locks in today an exchange rate for a currency exchange that will actually happen on a future date. Unlike FX Spot ([Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md), settled T+1/T+2), a Forward's value date can be weeks or months out — useful for hedging a known future cash need without being exposed to rate movement between now and then.

```text
FX FORWARD  —  Bank Of Toronto needs USD 10,000,000 in 3 months
========================================================================

  Today's Spot Rate (USD/CAD)     : 1.3500   (1 USD = CAD 1.3500)
  3-Month Forward Points          : +0.0045  (driven by CAD-USD interest rate gap)
  3-Month Forward Rate            : 1.3545   (1.3500 + 0.0045)

  Bank Of Toronto agrees TODAY with Goldman Sachs:
    In 3 months, Bank Of Toronto pays CAD 13,545,000
                 and receives USD 10,000,000
    (10,000,000 x 1.3545 = 13,545,000)

  -> No cash or currency moves today. Only the RATE is locked in now.

3 MONTHS LATER — value date arrives, two possible outcomes:
--------------------------------------------------------------
  If spot has moved to 1.3700 (USD got MORE expensive):
    Bank Of Toronto still only pays 13,545,000, not 13,700,000
    -> Forward SAVED Bank Of Toronto CAD 155,000 vs. buying at spot then.

  If spot has moved to 1.3400 (USD got CHEAPER):
    Bank Of Toronto still pays 13,545,000, even though spot would have
    only cost 13,400,000
    -> Forward COST Bank Of Toronto CAD 145,000 vs. buying at spot then.
       This is not "the trade going wrong" — it's the price of certainty:
       Bank Of Toronto hedged a known future need and gave up any chance
       of a better rate in exchange for eliminating the risk of a worse one.
```

**ISDA classification for this trade:** Asset Class = **Foreign Exchange**, Base Product = **Forward**, Sub-Product = **Deliverable** (both currencies physically exchange — contrast with an **NDF**, where only a cash difference settles because one currency isn't freely convertible), Transaction Type = **Forward**.

**Takeaway:** a Forward's Notional (USD 10,000,000 here) *does* eventually settle, unlike an Interest Rate Swap's notional — the difference is that a Forward is a promise to exchange two real currency amounts on one future date, not a stream of periodic net cash flows calculated off a reference number.

## 12. UPI — Unique Product Identifier: Theory & Worked Example

> **Deep dive available:** this section covers UPI (and UTI) at the level this curriculum needs going forward. For the full picture — UTI's structure and generation waterfall, UPI reference-data templates across all six asset classes, how ANNA-DSB actually operates (including its separate role issuing OTC-derivative ISINs for MiFIR), and a regime-by-regime map of where each identifier is required — see the standalone [UPI, UTI &amp; ANNA-DSB Deep Dive](./upi-uti-dsb-deep-dive.md).

**Definition:** A **UPI (Unique Product Identifier)** is a global, standardized identifier for an OTC derivative *product* — the closest thing the derivatives world has to an ISIN, but identifying a **product template**, not one specific trade. It's issued by the **DSB (Derivatives Service Bureau)**, a subsidiary of the same organization (ANNA) that coordinates ISIN issuance worldwide, under technical guidance from **CPMI-IOSCO**. Since roughly 2022–2024, regulators in most major jurisdictions (CFTC, EMIR/UK EMIR rewrites, and others — see the Abbreviations Glossary §9) have made the UPI a *mandatory* field on OTC derivative trade reports, specifically to replace the patchwork of internal, firm-specific product codes that made it impossible for regulators to compare data across firms.

The critical thing that trips people up: **UPI identifies the product, not the trade, and not a specific counterparty pair.** This is easiest to see by putting all four identifier types side by side:

| Identifier | What it identifies | Unique per… | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ISIN** | A specific cash security | One instrument, referenced by many trades | `US0378331005` (Apple common stock) |
| **UPI** | A derivative *product template* (asset class + type + underlier + key economic terms) | One product, referenced by many trades — even across different counterparties | One UPI for "5Y CAD Fixed-Float IRS referencing CORRA" |
| **UTI** | One specific derivative *transaction* | Exactly one trade, generated at execution | A brand-new UTI every time Bank Of Toronto executes a swap |
| **Trade ID** | A firm's own internal transaction reference | Exactly one trade, but only meaningful inside that firm's own systems | `BOT-2026-000514` |

Notice the pattern mirrors Section 01's hierarchy almost exactly: UPI sits at roughly the "Product/Instrument" level (reference data, looked up once, reused constantly), while UTI and Trade ID sit at the "Trade" level (a fresh value every single time). The difference from cash instruments is that a derivative doesn't get its own ISIN-like "one instrument, many trades" identifier at the *instrument* level, because — as Section 07 explained — a derivative has no existence independent of the two parties' contract. UPI fills that structural gap at the *product* level instead, one rung higher than where an ISIN would sit.

```text
UPI vs UTI  —  WHERE EACH SITS IN THE HIERARCHY
====================================================================

  ASSET CLASS -> PRODUCT -> [ no instrument-level ID for OTC derivatives ] -> TRADE
                      |                                                          |
                      v                                                          v
              UPI identifies                                        UTI identifies
              the PRODUCT TEMPLATE                                  ONE TRANSACTION
              (reused across trades)                                 (new every time)
```

### How a UPI is actually built

A UPI is a 12-character code (formatted like an ISIN), but the code itself is just a lookup key — the real content lives in the **UPI Reference Data** record behind it, a standardized set of fields describing the product: Asset Class, Instrument Type, Underlier(s), Notional Currency, Delivery Type, and a handful of other economic terms specific to that instrument type. Two derivative trades generate **the same UPI** if and only if every one of those product-defining fields matches — the trade-specific details (notional amount, price/rate, counterparty, trade date) are deliberately **not** part of what defines a UPI, which is exactly why the same UPI can legitimately appear on thousands of unrelated trades.

### Worked example — one UPI, three different Interest Rate Swap trades

Revisiting the 5Y CAD Interest Rate Swap product from Section 08 — suppose Bank Of Toronto executes three separate swaps over a few months, all sharing the same product shape (5Y tenor, CAD notional, fixed-vs-CORRA-floating) but with completely different trade-specific details:

```text
ONE PRODUCT (ONE UPI) -- THREE UNRELATED TRADES (THREE UTIs)
========================================================================

  UPI: QZ9F4K2XR7T1   (illustrative — issued by the DSB)
  Product definition behind this UPI:
    Asset Class      : Interest Rate
    Instrument Type  : Swap, Fixed-Float
    Underlier        : CORRA
    Notional Currency: CAD
    Tenor             : 5 Years

  TRADE A                         TRADE B                         TRADE C
  Trade ID : BOT-2026-000514      Trade ID : BOT-2026-000601      Trade ID : BOT-2026-000725
  UTI      : CA1B0T20260817001    UTI      : CA1B0T20260903002    UTI      : CA1B0T20261014003
  UPI      : QZ9F4K2XR7T1  <-SAME UPI as Trade A and Trade B, same product
  Counterparty : Goldman Sachs    Counterparty : Maple Leaf Fund   Counterparty : Goldman Sachs
  Notional : CAD 100,000,000      Notional : CAD 35,000,000        Notional : CAD 250,000,000
  Fixed Rate : 4.00%              Fixed Rate : 3.85%                Fixed Rate : 4.15%
  Trade Date : 17-Aug-2026        Trade Date : 03-Sep-2026          Trade Date : 14-Oct-2026
```

**Takeaway:** all three rows above are completely different trades with different counterparties, notionals, rates, and dates — each gets its own unique UTI and its own internal Trade ID, exactly as expected at the Trade layer. But because all three share the identical product shape (5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swap), they all report the **same UPI**. If you ever see a reporting query group thousands of distinct trades under one UPI value, that's not a data quality bug — it's the UPI doing exactly what it was designed to do: let a regulator aggregate "how much 5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swap risk exists across the whole market" without having to understand every firm's internal product coding scheme.

## 13. Worked Example — Interest Rate Swap (IRS)

**Definition:** an IRS is an agreement to exchange a **fixed** stream of interest payments for a **floating** stream, both calculated off the same **Notional** ([Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md) §8), which itself never changes hands. Unlike Section 9's CDS (a contingent payout that may never happen) or Section 10's FX Forward (two real currency amounts that eventually settle), an IRS's notional **never settles at all** — only a small net difference moves, and it moves **every single period**, not once at maturity.

```text
INTEREST RATE SWAP  —  Bank Of Toronto pays fixed, receives floating vs CORRA
================================================================================

  Notional          : CAD 100,000,000  (reference only — never exchanged)
  Fixed Rate         : 4.00% (Bank Of Toronto pays)
  Floating Rate      : 3-month CORRA + 0.05% spread (Goldman Sachs pays)
  Term               : 5 years, quarterly payment dates (20 settlements total)

SCENARIO A — CORRA averages 3.20% this quarter (the normal case)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
  Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 3.25% x (91/365) = CAD 810,068
                                       (3.20% CORRA + 0.05% spread)

  Bank Of Toronto  ---- pays CAD 187,192 net ---->  Goldman Sachs

SCENARIO B — CORRA instead averages 4.30% this quarter (rates moved up)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
  Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 4.35% x (91/365) = CAD 1,084,521
                                       (4.30% CORRA + 0.05% spread)

  Goldman Sachs  ---- pays CAD 87,261 net ---->  Bank Of Toronto
  (the direction FLIPS — same trade, same two parties, opposite net payer,
  purely because CORRA reset higher this quarter)

  -> This repeats every quarter for 5 years — 20 separate net settlements,
     each independently determined by that period's own CORRA reset. The
     CAD 100,000,000 notional itself never moves on ANY of those 20 dates.
```

**ISDA classification for this trade:** Asset Class = **Interest Rate**, Base Product = **IRSwap**, Sub-Product = **FixedFloat**, Transaction Type = **Swap**. This is the exact four-level label behind the "Interest Rate / IRSwap / FixedFloat / Swap" shorthand already used in Section 11's cross-reference table.

**Takeaway:** put side by side with Sections 9 and 10, this trade completes a genuinely useful three-way contrast in "what actually settles": an IRS's notional **never** settles (only a net difference, repeatedly); an FX Forward's notional **does** eventually settle (both full currency amounts, once, Section 10); a CDS's notional **may never** settle at all (a contingent lump sum, only if a credit event occurs, Section 9). Three OTC derivatives, three completely different relationships between "Notional" and "what actually moves in cash" — exactly the kind of field-level nuance a reporting system has to encode per product, not assume uniformly.

## Answers — Recap Quiz, with Explanations

**1. New market value = CAD 24,625,000. Quantity and Value changed; Price, Currency, Instrument, and all reference-data fields stayed the same.**

```text
Market Value = 25,000,000 x 98.50 / 100 = CAD 24,625,000
```

Quantity (Trade field) scales the value directly. Price (98.50) didn't change because it's the market's price for the instrument at that moment, independent of how much any one buyer wants. Everything above the Trade layer — Asset Class, Product, Instrument, ISIN — is identical, because it's still the same bond.

**2. More likely a bond.**

Tickers exist for exchange-traded instruments, which in practice means mainly equities. A CUSIP with no Ticker is the classic signature of a bond record — bonds get identifiers (CUSIP/ISIN) but never trade under an exchange ticker the way shares do.

**3. Because one is Quantity (something real that settles) and the other is Notional (a reference number used only for calculation).**

The CAD 100,000,000 bond purchase means CAD 100,000,000 face value of bonds actually gets delivered against CAD ~100,000,000 (give or take price) in cash at settlement — real securities and real cash move. The CAD 100,000,000 swap notional never moves at all; it only exists so the fixed and floating legs have a number to multiply against (Section 3, and [Module 1](./module-01-fundamentals.md) Section 8). Same displayed figure, completely different settlement reality.

**4. Trade is the only layer that's new every time. Asset Class, Product, and Instrument stay the same across many trades.**

A single instrument like "Canada 5% Bond 2035" can sit underneath thousands of separate Trade records over its lifetime — every one of those trades still points back to the same Asset Class (Fixed Income), Product (Government Bond), and Instrument (that one bond). Only the Trade layer captures who transacted, how much, at what price, and when.

**5. ISIN and CUSIP still work everywhere; Ticker likely would not.**

ISIN is globally unique by design and travels with the security regardless of which exchange it trades on. CUSIP is also unaffected by exchange venue (it identifies the security, not the listing). Ticker, however, is exchange-specific — Apple's ticker on a European exchange (e.g. Frankfurt's Xetra) is commonly shown as something like `APC`, not `AAPL`, because tickers are assigned per listing venue, not globally like ISIN/CUSIP.

**6. Payout = CAD 15,000,000. A lower recovery rate means bondholders/creditors recover less in the default, so the CDS has to cover a bigger loss.**

```text
Loss Given Default = Notional x (1 - Recovery Rate)
                    = 20,000,000 x (1 - 0.25)
                    = CAD 15,000,000
```

Recovery Rate represents how many cents on the dollar creditors get back after a default (e.g. from liquidating the defaulted company's remaining assets). Recovery Rate and CDS payout move in opposite directions: at 40% recovery the payout was CAD 12,000,000; dropping recovery to 25% means creditors recover even less of their own money, so the protection seller (Goldman Sachs) has to make up a bigger shortfall — CAD 15,000,000.

**7. Because ISDA Taxonomy classifies OTC derivative *contracts*, and a cash bond isn't a contract between two parties — it's a standalone security.**

A bond exists independently of who currently holds it; buying or selling one just transfers ownership of something that already existed (Section 6). An Interest Rate Swap only exists *because* two counterparties agreed to it — it has no existence outside that bilateral contract, which is exactly the kind of thing ISDA taxonomy was built to classify consistently across the industry (Section 7). Cash instruments use CFI codes and ISIN/CUSIP instead; they were never in ISDA's scope.

**8. ISDA Asset Class → Base Product → Sub-Product → Transaction Type. "Credit" sits at the first level (Asset Class); "CreditDefaultSwap" sits at the last level (Transaction Type).**

Using the Section 9 example: Asset Class = Credit, Base Product = SingleName, Sub-Product = Corporate, Transaction Type = CreditDefaultSwap. The taxonomy gets more specific at each level — "Credit" alone doesn't distinguish a CDS from a Total Return Swap, but by the fourth level, "CreditDefaultSwap" is unambiguous.

**9. Bad outcome (an opportunity cost) for Bank Of Toronto — the mirror image of the original example, because Bank Of Toronto is now on the selling side of USD instead of the buying side.**

```text
Bank Of Toronto sells USD 10,000,000 forward at locked rate 1.3545:
  Receives CAD 13,545,000 in 3 months, regardless of where spot lands.

If spot later moves to 1.3700 (USD got MORE expensive):
  Selling USD at that HIGHER spot rate would fetch MORE CAD:
  10,000,000 x 1.3700 = CAD 13,700,000 (spot) vs CAD 13,545,000 (forward)
  -> Forward COST Bank Of Toronto CAD 155,000 vs. waiting and selling at spot.
```

This is exactly the mirror of the original buyer scenario: in Section 10, Bank Of Toronto (the USD *buyer*) benefited when spot rose above the locked forward rate, because buying gets cheaper as the forward looks better by comparison. Here, as the USD *seller*, Bank Of Toronto is hurt when spot rises above the locked forward rate, because selling later at the higher spot would have fetched more CAD. Whether a rate move "helps" or "hurts" a forward always depends on which side of the currency pair you locked in — buyer and seller face exactly opposite exposures to the same rate movement.

**10. Their UTI and Trade ID are different. Sharing a UPI only means the two trades have the identical *product shape* — it says nothing about counterparty, size, rate, or date, none of which are part of a UPI's definition.**

```text
Trade A: UTI CA1B0T20260817001, Trade ID BOT-2026-000514, Notional CAD 100,000,000, Rate 4.00%, 17-Aug-2026
Trade C: UTI CA1B0T20261014003, Trade ID BOT-2026-000725, Notional CAD 250,000,000, Rate 4.15%, 14-Oct-2026
```

A UPI is deliberately built only from product-defining fields (Asset Class, Instrument Type, Underlier, Notional Currency, Tenor) — Section 12 lists exactly which fields those are, and counterparty/notional/rate/date are not among them. That's by design: the UPI's job is to let a regulator group "all 5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swaps" together across the whole market, which only works if trade-specific details are excluded from what defines it. UTI (and, internally, Trade ID) exist precisely to carry the trade-specific identity that UPI intentionally leaves out — every real transaction gets a fresh one, even when a thousand other transactions share its exact product template.

**11. Net settlement = CAD 0 — but that does NOT mean nothing happens on that date.**

```text
Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
                                     (3.95% CORRA + 0.05% spread)
Net = 997,260 - 997,260 = CAD 0
```

Both legs still get independently calculated off that quarter's CORRA reset — the netting step just happens to produce a zero result this particular quarter, purely by coincidence of where CORRA landed. The trade doesn't pause, terminate, or skip processing; the next quarter's settlement is calculated completely independently, off whatever CORRA does next. A reporting system that only books an entry when net cash actually moves would silently miss recording this period's settlement altogether — the correct behavior is to always compute and report both legs and the net, even when the net happens to equal zero.
