01 · A physical good, not a financial claim

What Is Commodities? — Theory & Terminology

A Commodity is a physical, fungible good — one barrel of WTI crude is interchangeable with any other barrel of the same grade, the same way one CAD is interchangeable with any other CAD (Module 6 §1), but unlike a share (Module 4 §1) where each certificate represents a specific claim on a specific company. Commodities span Energy, Metals, and Agriculture — and almost nobody in capital markets actually wants the physical good. They want the price exposure.

TermMeaning
Spot PriceToday's price for near-immediate physical delivery — same concept as FX Spot (Module 6 §1), applied to a barrel of oil or an ounce of gold
Futures CurveThe set of futures prices across different delivery months for the same commodity — plotted, it shows the market's term structure
ContangoFutures curve slopes upward — later delivery months cost more than spot, typically because storage + financing costs exceed any convenience yield
BackwardationFutures curve slopes downward — later delivery months cost less than spot, typically when the commodity is scarce right now and holders pay a premium for immediate supply
Convenience YieldThe non-monetary benefit of physically holding a commodity now — offsets storage cost in the contango/backwardation math
RollClosing a futures position nearing expiry and opening the same position in a later-dated contract, to avoid ever taking physical delivery
Diagram · Contango vs BackwardationSame model as FX forward points
CONTANGO (upward-sloping curve):
  Spot: USD 75.00/bbl   1M Future: USD 75.60   3M Future: USD 76.90
  -> storage + financing cost dominates; later delivery costs more

BACKWARDATION (downward-sloping curve):
  Spot: USD 75.00/bbl   1M Future: USD 74.30   3M Future: USD 72.80
  -> near-term scarcity dominates; later delivery costs less

-> Exactly the FX forward-points mechanic from Module 6 §1, just
   driven by storage/convenience yield instead of an interest
   rate differential between two currencies.
Takeaway. The single hardest habit to unlearn coming from Equity or Fixed Income is treating a commodity price like a stock price. A stock's forward price is almost entirely driven by the risk-free rate (Module 10: Valuation); a commodity's forward price is driven by storage cost, financing cost, and convenience yield — three factors a share or a bond simply doesn't have.

02 · One real thing, three contracts referencing its price

What Are The Products? — Physical, Futures, Options & Swaps

What's new for Commodities is that the "real thing" itself is genuinely inconvenient to hold — which is exactly why Section 03 spends more time than any prior module explaining why almost everyone trades the contract, never the barrel.

Diagram · Commodity Product TreePhysical vs derivative
COMMODITIES
│
├── Physical Commodities   (the actual barrel, bar, or bushel — rare in a bank's own book)
├── Commodity Futures      (exchange-traded, standardized, obligation to buy/sell at a future date)
├── Commodity Options      (the RIGHT, not obligation, on a commodity future or physical price)
└── Commodity Swaps        (periodic net cash flows referencing a commodity price index — Module 2 §7)
Cash / DerivativeProductWhat it is
CASHPhysical CommodityThe actual tangible good — a 100oz Gold Bar, a barrel of crude, a tonne of wheat — bought and sold with physical delivery, storage, and title transfer
DERIVATIVECommodity FutureA standardized, exchange-traded contract to buy/sell a set quantity of a commodity at a fixed price on a fixed future date — almost always closed out or rolled before delivery (Section 01)
DERIVATIVECommodity OptionA contract giving the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) a commodity future at a fixed strike, for a premium — same asymmetric payoff as Module 4 §4d and Module 6 §2
DERIVATIVECommodity SwapTwo counterparties exchange cash flows over a period — one side pays a fixed price, the other pays a floating price referencing a published index (e.g. ICE Brent) — reusing Module 2 §7's 1Y Brent Crude Oil Swap
A fifth product, briefly: commodities also include the Commodity Warrant — an exchange-listed, long-dated call option issued by a bank (not written bilaterally the way Section 04d's option is) — economically closest to this section's Option, but issued and distributed more like a security. It's a niche product next to the other four and isn't covered with its own worked example here.
Takeaway. Notice the pattern already established across three asset classes now (Module 4 §2 Equity, Module 6 §2 FX): one "real thing" and a family of contracts referencing its price. What's new for Commodities is that the "real thing" itself is genuinely inconvenient to hold — exactly why Section 03 explains why almost everyone trades the contract, never the barrel.

03 · A third pattern: tangible, but almost never touched

What Exactly Is Being Traded? — Physical Good vs Price-Referencing Contract

Unlike Equity (Module 4: security vs. contract) or FX (Module 6: contracts, no security at all), Commodities sit in a third pattern: the underlying is real and tangible, like a security — but it's a good, not a financial claim, and almost never actually changes hands in a bank's trading book.

Diagram · Physical vs DerivativeTangible good vs price-referencing contract
Physical Gold (TANGIBLE GOOD):
  A 100oz Gold Bar, LBMA "Good Delivery" standard, sitting in a
  vault. It exists independently of any trade, like Coca-Cola
  stock (Module 4 §3) -- but unlike a share, it has WEIGHT,
  PURITY, a physical LOCATION, and costs money just to store
  and insure.

Commodity Future / Swap (CONTRACT):
  Bank Of Toronto enters a 1Y Brent Crude Oil Swap, notional
  100,000 barrels (the exact trade from Module 2 §7) -- no oil
  is bought, stored, or shipped. Only the DIFFERENCE between a
  fixed price and the floating ICE Brent index settles in cash,
  once a month, for the life of the swap.

-> Section 01's takeaway already flagged this: almost nobody in
   capital markets wants the physical commodity. A bank's own
   book is overwhelmingly Futures, Swaps, and Options; physical
   trading is a specialist activity sitting apart from the rest
   of the derivatives book.
Takeaway. This is why Module 2 §7's ISDA classification table flagged Commodity as the asset class where "physical settlement of the underlying is the rare exception, not the norm" — a stronger statement than Module 6 made about FX (where Spot does routinely settle physically) or Module 5 made about bonds (which always settle physically, just via book-entry). Commodities is the asset class where physical settlement is actively designed around.

04 · Buyer → Seller → Trade

Real-World Trade Examples

Four trades — Physical Gold, a WTI Future roll, a Brent Swap, and an Option hedge.

4a. Physical Commodity

Physical Gold · 100oz BarLBMA Good Delivery
Buyer:  Bank Of Toronto (precious metals desk)
Seller: A LBMA-accredited refiner

Trade: 100 oz @ Spot Price USD 2,650.00/oz
Trade Value = 100 x 2,650.00 = USD 265,000
Settlement: physical delivery into an LBMA-approved vault +
            cash payment, T+2 (standard for the London gold
            market)

-> This IS one of the genuinely rare cases (Section 03) where a
   bank actually takes physical delivery -- precious metals
   desks are one of the few areas of a bank that routinely does.

4b. Commodity Future

CME WTI Crude Oil Future (CL)1,000 bbl/contract
Buyer:  Bank Of Toronto (proprietary energy desk)
Seller: Counterparty on the CME order book (anonymous, novated
        to the CCP at execution -- Section 07)

Trade: BUY 50 contracts @ USD 75.50/bbl, front-month expiry
Notional = 50 x 1,000 x 75.50 = USD 3,775,000

ONE MONTH LATER, before expiry -- Bank Of Toronto ROLLS:
  SELLS 50 front-month contracts @ USD 76.10/bbl
    Realized P&L = 50 x 1,000 x (76.10 - 75.50) = +USD 30,000
  BUYS 50 next-month contracts @ USD 76.90/bbl (contango,
    Section 01 -- next month costs more)

-> Bank Of Toronto never took delivery of a single barrel --
   exactly the "roll" mechanic Section 01 defined, and the
   reason futures desks watch the contango/backwardation shape
   of the curve as closely as an FX desk watches forward points.

4c. Commodity Swap

1Y Brent Crude Oil SwapNotional 100,000 bbl — reused from Module 2 §7
Bank Of Toronto:  pays FIXED, receives FLOATING (ICE Brent index)
Counterparty:      Shell Trading

Fixed Price agreed today: USD 78.00/bbl
Monthly notional: 100,000 barrels / 12 = 8,333 barrels per month

MONTH 1 SETTLEMENT -- ICE Brent monthly average settles at USD 81.20/bbl
  Floating leg owed to Bank Of Toronto = 8,333 x 81.20 = USD 676,640
  Fixed leg owed by Bank Of Toronto    = 8,333 x 78.00 = USD 649,974
  Net cash flow: Bank Of Toronto RECEIVES USD 26,666
  (81.20 - 78.00) x 8,333 = USD 26,666 -- only the NET amount
  moves, exactly the IRS netting mechanic from Module 1 §8

-> No oil changes hands at any point across the full 12 months --
   this is Module 2 §7's "Commodity / Energy / Oil / Swap" entry
   made concrete with real monthly cash flows.

4d. Commodity Option

Call on CME WTI Crude Oil FutureStrike USD 80.00/bbl
Buyer:  Bank Of Toronto's client (an airline hedging jet fuel cost,
        using WTI as a proxy hedge)
Seller (writer): Bank Of Toronto

Trade: WTI Call, Strike USD 80.00/bbl, Notional 100,000 bbl
       (100 contracts x 1,000 bbl), Expiry 6 months
       Premium: USD 3.20/bbl
Premium Paid = 100,000 x 3.20 = USD 320,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario A: WTI Future settles at USD 88.00/bbl
  Option is in-the-money: exercising saves
    (88.00 - 80.00) x 100,000 = USD 800,000 vs. buying at market
  Net Benefit = 800,000 - 320,000 (premium) = USD 480,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario B: WTI Future settles at USD 74.00/bbl
  Option expires WORTHLESS -- buying at market (74.00) is
  cheaper than exercising at strike (80.00)
  Maximum Loss = the USD 320,000 premium, exactly as Module 4
  §4d and Module 6 §4d established -- never more than premium paid.
Takeaway. Four trades, four very different settlement realities — 4a is the rare case that actually moves a physical good; 4b, 4c, and 4d never do. This is the single most important operational fact a reporting system needs to encode correctly for Commodities: the product type alone determines whether "settlement" means a vault delivery or a cash netting.

05 · Clearing happens at execution, not after

Trade Lifecycle — Commodity-Specific Walkthrough

Order → Execution → Trade Capture → Confirmation → Clearing → Settlement → Position → Valuation/P&L → Reporting, applied to the Commodity Future trade from Section 04b.

Full Lifecycle · CME WTI Crude FutureBOT-CMD-2026-027715
09:31:00  ORDER — Bank Of Toronto's energy desk instructed to buy
          50 front-month WTI contracts

09:31:04  EXECUTION @ USD 75.50/bbl on CME's order book

09:31:04  TRADE CAPTURE — Trade ID BOT-CMD-2026-027715

09:31:05  CLEARING — trade NOVATED to CME Clearing (the CCP)
          immediately at execution -- unlike Module 6's bilateral
          FX or Module 5's bilateral bonds, an exchange-traded
          future is centrally cleared from the moment it trades
          (Section 07)

DAILY     VARIATION MARGIN — CME Clearing marks the position to
          the daily settlement price and moves cash EVERY DAY,
          not just at expiry:
            Day 1 settle USD 76.00: Bank Of Toronto receives
              50 x 1,000 x (76.00 - 75.50) = USD 25,000
            Day 2 settle USD 75.20: Bank Of Toronto pays
              50 x 1,000 x (76.00 - 75.20) = USD 40,000
          -> this daily cash movement (Module 11: Risk & Exposure)
             is a structural feature of exchange-traded futures
             that OTC swaps (Section 04c) don't have unless a CSA
             requires daily variation margin too (Module 12)

POSITION — Bank Of Toronto's Commodity book shows 50 WTI
          contracts, front-month, until rolled or closed

VALUATION / P&L — mark-to-market daily against the CME
          settlement price; because variation margin already
          moved the cash, P&L and cash flow are the SAME number
          every day for an exchange-traded future -- a genuine
          simplification versus Fixed Income's split between
          price P&L and accrual (Module 5 §5)

REPORTING — exchange-traded futures are reported to the CFTC
          (Section 08) via the exchange/clearing house itself,
          not via a bilateral UTI/UPI the way an OTC Commodity
          Swap (Section 04c) would be
Takeaway. The biggest lifecycle difference from every prior module is when clearing happens. Equity (Module 4) and Fixed Income (Module 5) clear via a CCP but usually a step after execution; FX (Module 6) mostly never clears via a CCP at all. An exchange-traded Commodity Future is novated to the CCP essentially instantaneously, and that CCP relationship is what drives daily variation margin.

06 · Commodity codes, contract codes, UPI & UTI

Identifiers — Commodity Codes, Exchange Contracts, UPI & UTI

Commodities split cleanly along the Section 03 physical/contract line: physical goods get commodity codes, not securities identifiers; exchange-traded futures get exchange contract codes; OTC swaps and options get UPI/UTI exactly like Module 6's FX derivatives.

IdentifierApplies to Commodities?Example
ISIN / CUSIP / SEDOL / Ticker / FIGINo for Physical or OTC Swaps — these identify securities, and neither is one (Section 03)—
Commodity CodeYes, for physical trades — an internal/industry classification, not a security identifierXAU (gold, ISO 4217-style commodity code)
Exchange Contract CodeYes, for exchange-traded Futures/Options — identifies the specific contract and delivery monthCLZ26 (CME WTI Crude, December 2026 expiry)
UPIYes, for OTC Commodity Swaps and Options reported as OTC derivatives (Module 2 §12)One UPI for "Brent Crude, Swap, Cash-Settled" — reused across many swap trades, same logic as Module 6 §6's FX Forward UPI
UTIYes — every individual OTC Commodity Swap/Option trade gets its ownA fresh UTI per trade, same as Module 6 §6
LEIYes — identifies Bank Of Toronto, Shell Trading, and any client as counterpartiesEntity, not instrument (Module 4 §6)
Takeaway. A Commodity trade record with an exchange contract code (like CLZ26) but no UPI/UTI is a Future — centrally cleared, exchange-identified, no bilateral reporting IDs needed. A record with a UPI/UTI but no exchange contract code is an OTC Swap or Option. Seeing both populated on the same trade, or neither populated at all, is the Commodities-specific version of the diagnostic checks Module 5 §6 and Module 6 §6 already established.

07 · The first asset class that genuinely splits down the middle

Exchange vs OTC vs Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared

Commodities is the first asset class in this roadmap where the exchange-traded, centrally-cleared product (Futures) is genuinely the dominant, most liquid way most participants get commodity exposure — closer to Module 4's Equity default than to Module 5's Fixed Income or Module 6's FX defaults. Physical trades and OTC Swaps/Options remain bilateral.

Cash / DerivativeProductExchange or OTC?Bilateral or Centrally Cleared?
CASHPhysical CommodityOTC (dealer-to-dealer, or dealer-to-refiner/producer)Bilateral (the norm — Section 04a)
DERIVATIVECommodity FutureExchange-traded (CME, ICE, LME)Centrally cleared from execution (Section 05)
DERIVATIVECommodity OptionMostly exchange-traded (options ON futures); some bespoke OTCCentrally cleared if exchange-listed; bilateral if OTC (Section 04d's airline hedge is OTC-style, bespoke strike/expiry)
DERIVATIVECommodity SwapOTCMostly bilateral; a growing share clears via ICE Clear or LCH for standardized benchmarks (e.g. Brent, WTI)
Takeaway. Unlike every other module's single dominant default, Commodities genuinely splits down the middle by product — Futures behave like Module 4's Equity (exchange, centrally cleared, novated instantly), while Physical and most Swaps behave like Module 5's Fixed Income or Module 6's FX (OTC, bilateral). Knowing which Commodity product a trade record represents is the single fastest way to predict its whole downstream lifecycle.

08 · The first module where the CFTC leads

Regulatory Perspective — MiFID, EMIR, SFTR, FCA, CIRO, HKMA, DTCC, DMO

The CFTC is literally named for this asset class — a direct consequence of Section 07's finding that exchange-traded Futures are the dominant product.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Commodities specifically
MiFID II / MiFIR (EU)Covers commodity derivatives directly, with position limits on certain commodity derivative contracts — a control mechanism Equity, Fixed Income, and FX don't have an equivalent of in this roadmap so far
EMIRApplies to OTC Commodity Swaps and Options as derivatives (Module 2 §07) — but generally not to a physical forward used for genuine commercial delivery purposes (an "ancillary activity" carve-out similar in spirit to Module 6 §08's FX Spot/Forward boundary, though defined differently)
SFTRNot applicable to Commodities the way it is to repo (Module 5 §08) — commodities lending exists but is a narrow, specialist niche, not a Commodities-module-defining product
FCA (UK)Conduct regulator for UK commodity derivatives trading; London is also home to the LME (London Metal Exchange), making the FCA's oversight of metals trading specifically significant, similar in spirit to Module 6 §08's FX-volume point
CIRO (Canada)SRO overseeing Canadian commodity derivatives dealer conduct
HKMA (Hong Kong)Genuinely more of a footnote here than in FX (Module 6 §08) or Fixed Income (Module 5 §08) — the SFC and HKEX (which absorbed the LME in 2012) are the more relevant Hong Kong-linked bodies for this asset class
CFTC (US)The single most consequential regulator for this specific module — the Commodity Futures Trading Commission is literally named for this asset class, sets swap data reporting, clearing, and SEF trading rules under Dodd-Frank, and enforces the position limits MiFID also uses in the EU
DTCCIts ICE Trade Vault (an SDR) accepts commodity derivative reports alongside interest rate and credit products, feeding CFTC reporting requirements
DMO (UK Debt Management Office)Not Commodities-relevant — issues Gilts (Module 5 §08), included here for completeness only
Takeaway. Commodities is the first module where the CFTC, not a European or UK body, is clearly the headline regulator — a direct consequence of Section 07's finding that Futures are the dominant product, and the largest commodity futures exchanges (CME, ICE) sit under CFTC jurisdiction. This mirrors exactly how Module 6 §08 flagged the FCA as outsized for FX because of London's FX volume — same pattern, different regulator, different asset class.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — The roll would cost LESS (or even generate a credit) in backwardation, versus a real cost in contango
Math · Roll Cost, Contango vs BackwardationWTI Future
Contango roll (worked example): sell front-month at 76.10, buy
next-month at 76.90 -> pay an extra 0.80/bbl to roll forward

Backwardation roll (this question): sell front-month at 76.10,
buy next-month at 74.80 -> RECEIVE an extra 1.30/bbl to roll forward

In contango, rolling forward always means buying the more expensive later-dated contract — a recurring cost to staying long via futures. In backwardation, rolling forward means buying a cheaper later-dated contract — a recurring benefit. This is exactly why "roll yield" is a real, closely watched P&L driver for anyone holding futures long-term rather than taking delivery, tying directly back to Section 01's contango/backwardation definitions.

Q2 — No cash flow would move at all — variation margin only moves when the settlement price CHANGES from the prior day's settlement price

Section 05's mechanic is a day-over-day comparison: Day 2 already marked the position to USD 75.20 and settled that difference in cash. If Day 3 settles at the same USD 75.20, there is zero incremental mark-to-market movement, so zero variation margin changes hands that day — the position's cumulative value hasn't moved, even though a full trading day has passed.

Q3 — Almost every Commodity product is structurally built so physical delivery never has to happen; Fixed Income settlement, by contrast, is always physical, just dematerialized into book-entry form

Section 03 states this directly: physical settlement is "the rare exception, not the norm" for Commodities, while Module 5 §3 never suggested a bond trade could avoid moving the actual (book-entry) security — a bond trade's DvP settlement (Module 1 §7) always transfers the real security, it's just no longer a paper certificate.

Q4 — That combination is a red flag, not a healthy pattern — it most likely indicates a data-quality error

Section 06 establishes that exchange contract codes identify centrally-cleared Futures (which don't need bilateral UTI/UPI reporting) while UPI/UTI identify OTC derivatives reported bilaterally. A record should have either an exchange contract code (Future) or a UPI/UTI (OTC Swap/Option), not both — the Commodities-specific version of the same diagnostic pattern Module 5 §6 flagged for bonds carrying a UPI, and Module 6 §6 flagged for the reverse.

Because Bank Of Toronto pays fixed and receives floating (Section 04c), whenever the floating index settles below the fixed price, Bank Of Toronto is the net payer — the mirror image of the worked example, where a higher floating settlement made Bank Of Toronto the net receiver. This is the same fixed-vs-floating netting logic Module 1 §8 established for interest rate swaps.

Q6 — Because exchange-traded Futures, which sit under CFTC jurisdiction, are the dominant, most liquid Commodity product

Section 08's takeaway draws this connection explicitly: the CFTC's outsized relevance is a direct consequence of Commodities being the first asset class where the centrally-cleared, exchange-traded product is the default rather than the exception — and the largest commodity futures exchanges (CME, ICE) are US-regulated, unlike Fixed Income or FX where the dominant product was OTC and bilateral.

Q7 — A Future clears instantaneously because it's exchange-traded by design, with the exchange's own CCP standing behind every trade; FX mostly never clears via a CCP because it's overwhelmingly OTC and bilateral, settled instead via CLS's PvP
This is the same structural split Section 07 draws out for Commodities itself (Futures vs. Physical/Swaps) applied across asset classes: whether a trade clears centrally and how fast depends entirely on whether the product is exchange-traded by design, not on some asset-class-wide rule — Commodities happens to have both patterns coexisting within a single asset class, where FX (Module 6) and Fixed Income (Module 5) are almost entirely one pattern each.
Q8 — CDS/CDCC only clear/settle products actually traded on Canadian venues; a CME future clears entirely within the US ecosystem regardless of the trading bank's home country

Bank Of Toronto's Canadian entity is just a remote participant in CME/CME Clearing via an FCM relationship, with Canadian involvement limited to CIRO's conduct oversight, not settlement infrastructure. The NGX swap, by contrast, trades AND clears on Canadian-domiciled infrastructure (NGX / NGX Clearing). Section 9a's table states the general rule directly: Canadian market infrastructure only activates when the venue itself is Canadian, not merely because the trading bank happens to be Canadian — the nationality of the counterparty and the nationality of the clearing/settlement chain are two entirely independent facts about a trade.
