01 · Three motivations, same instruments

What Is Hedging? — Theory & the Three Trading Motivations

Hedging means deliberately taking a second position whose value moves opposite to an existing risk, so a loss on one side is offset by a gain on the other. The goal is narrowing the range of outcomes, not making money on the hedge itself.

Hedging, speculation, and arbitrage all use the same instruments this curriculum already built. What distinguishes them isn't the trade's shape — it's whether the trader already has an offsetting exposure.

MotivationExisting offsetting exposure?GoalExample
HedgingYesReduce the range of outcomesModule 15 §4c: Bank Of Toronto sells T-Note futures against bonds it already owns
SpeculationNo — new directional betProfit from a viewModule 4 §4c/§4d: Maple Leaf Pension Fund buys E-mini futures & an AAPL call to gain new exposure
ArbitrageN/ANear risk-free profit from a price gapNot covered elsewhere in this curriculum
Same Instrument, Opposite MotivationModule 15 vs. Module 4
Module 15 §4c:  Bank Of Toronto SELLS 100 CME 10-Year T-Note
                futures -- because it already OWNS bonds whose
                value falls when rates rise. The future's loss/gain
                is designed to CANCEL OUT the bond book's gain/loss.
                -> THIS IS A HEDGE.

Module 4 §4c:   Maple Leaf Pension Fund BUYS 20 E-mini S&P 500
                futures -- with no existing short equity position
                anywhere being offset. It's taking on NEW market
                exposure on purpose.
                -> THIS IS SPECULATION / deliberate exposure-taking.

-> Both are FUTURES. Nothing about the CONTRACT TYPE tells you
   which one is the hedge -- only knowing what ELSE the trader
   holds does.
Takeaway. A reporting or risk system that infers "is this a hedge?" purely from instrument type will get it wrong every time — hedging is a relationship between two positions, not a property of one trade in isolation. Section 9 covers how systems capture that relationship formally.

02 · Not risk-free

The Core Mechanic — Offsetting P&L, Not Eliminating Risk

Every hedge pairs two positions whose P&L moves opposite when the same risk factor moves. The combined P&L line is flatter than either position alone — that flattening is the hedge.

The Generic Shape of Every Hedge
UNDERLYING POSITION P&L:   loses money as risk factor moves one way,
                            gains as it moves the other way
      (e.g. bond value falls as rates rise)

HEDGE POSITION P&L:        moves the OPPOSITE way to the underlying
      (e.g. short future GAINS as rates rise)

COMBINED P&L:              flatter than either leg alone -- some
                            residual movement usually remains
                            (basis risk, Section 7), but the SWINGS
                            are smaller than holding the underlying
                            position unhedged

Hedging is never free. The cost shows up as either opportunity cost (a symmetric hedge locks in an outcome, giving up upside along with downside) or an explicit premium (an asymmetric hedge costs money upfront, like insurance, protecting only the downside).

Takeaway. "We hedged the position" does not mean the risk disappeared — it means the range of outcomes narrowed, at a cost. A reporting system showing a hedged position's P&L flat-lining to exactly zero every time is almost certainly wrong — basis risk is the normal case.

03 · The core practical choice

Two Families of Hedge Instruments — Symmetric vs. Asymmetric

Every hedge instrument in this curriculum falls into one of two families, and the choice between them is the single most important practical decision a hedger makes.

Symmetric hedgesAsymmetric hedges
InstrumentsForwards, Futures, SwapsOptions
Upfront costNone (futures need margin, not premium)A premium, paid upfront
Payoff shapeLocks in one outcome — dollar-for-dollar either directionOne-sided — protects the bad direction, keeps the good
Max loss on hedge itselfUnbounded in the "wrong" direction (by design)Capped at the premium paid
AnalogyA fixed-price contract — locked in, for better or worseAn insurance policy
Takeaway. Section 6 puts both families side by side against the exact same underlying need — the clearest illustration that this is a real choice with real trade-offs, not two products that just happen to both be called "hedges."

04 · Duration/DV01 matching

Worked Example — Interest Rate Hedging (Duration/DV01)

Reusing Module 10 §3's DV01 for Bank Of Toronto's Canada 5% Bond 2035, and Module 15 §4c's short T-Note futures hedge against that same book.

Interest Rate HedgeBond Book vs. T-Note Futures
THE EXPOSURE (Module 10 §3):
  Canada 5% Bond 2035 position
  Modified Duration: 7.2 | Market Value: CAD 9,850,000
  DV01 = CAD 7,092 per basis point
  -> if rates RISE, this bond position LOSES roughly CAD 7,092
     for every 1bp of rate increase

THE HEDGE (Module 15 §4c):
  Bank Of Toronto SELLS 100 CME 10-Year T-Note futures @ 112'16
  -> if rates RISE, bond futures prices FALL, and a SHORT position
     GAINS -- moving opposite to the bond book's loss
  Next business day, price falls to 111'24: Variation Margin
  GAIN = USD 75,000, credited to Bank Of Toronto's margin account,
  offsetting the loss on the actual bond portfolio being hedged

Sizing the hedge — the "hedge ratio" question. Not a notional-matching exercise — a bond and a bond future don't have identical price sensitivity to a rate move. The correct approach matches risk sensitivities, not face amounts:

Hedge Ratio — General FormulaConceptual
Number of futures contracts needed  ~  Exposure's DV01
                                        -----------------------
                                        Future's DV01 per contract

-> the goal is for the FUTURES POSITION'S total DV01 to
   approximately match the BOND POSITION'S DV01 (CAD 7,092/bp
   here), so a given rate move produces roughly equal and
   opposite dollar moves on each side.
Takeaway. Interest rate hedging is risk-sensitivity matching, not notional matching — Module 1 §8's "notional is a reference number, not what moves," applied to hedge sizing. Using face value instead of DV01 leaves a position under- or over-hedged without anyone realizing it until a large rate move exposes the gap.

05 · Exact reference-entity match

Worked Example — Credit Hedging (CDS Against Bond Credit Risk)

Reusing Module 2 §9's CDS in full — the cleanest hedge relationship in this curriculum, because the hedge instrument references the exact same reference entity as the exposure.

Credit HedgeCDS on Northern Rail Corp
THE HYPOTHETICAL EXPOSURE: Bank Of Toronto holds Northern Rail
Corp bonds (or has lent it money directly) -- if Northern Rail
Corp defaults, the bond loses value.

THE HEDGE (Module 2 §9, fully worked):
  Bank Of Toronto BUYS PROTECTION from Goldman Sachs
  Notional: CAD 20,000,000 | Spread: 120bps | Maturity: 5 years

  NO DEFAULT (normal case) -- Bank Of Toronto PAYS a quarterly
  premium: CAD 59,836 (the cost of the hedge, paid whether or
  not the bond ever defaults)

  DEFAULT OCCURS -- Goldman Sachs PAYS Bank Of Toronto:
    Loss Given Default = 20,000,000 x (1 - 40% recovery)
                        = CAD 12,000,000
  -> designed to roughly OFFSET the loss Bank Of Toronto
     simultaneously takes on the defaulted bond itself
Takeaway. Close to a "perfect" hedge because the reference-entity match is exact — but the CDS notional must still be sized to the bond exposure, and if Goldman Sachs (the protection seller) is itself more likely to fail exactly when Northern Rail Corp does, that's wrong-way risk: the hedge is least reliable exactly when it's needed most.

06 · Symmetric vs. asymmetric, head to head

Worked Example — FX Hedging, Two Ways (Forward vs. Option)

The same need — Bank Of Toronto's client must buy USD in three months — hedged two structurally different ways.

Same Need, Two Hedge ChoicesBuy USD in 3 Months
OPTION A -- FX FORWARD (Module 2 §10, symmetric, no premium)
  Locks in TODAY: pay CAD 13,545,000, receive USD 10,000,000,
  in 3 months, no matter what
  -> Spot to 1.3700 (USD costlier): Forward SAVED CAD 155,000
  -> Spot to 1.3400 (USD cheaper):  Forward COST CAD 145,000
  LOCKED IN EITHER WAY -- no premium, but no upside chance either

OPTION B -- FX OPTION (Module 6 §4d, asymmetric, premium paid)
  USD Call / CAD Put, Strike 1.3600, Notional USD 5,000,000
  Premium Paid: 5,000,000 x 0.0180 = CAD 90,000
  -> Spot to 1.3800 (USD costlier): EXERCISE, saves CAD 100,000
     vs. spot; net benefit after premium = CAD 10,000
  -> Spot to 1.3400 (USD cheaper): let it EXPIRE, buy at the
     cheaper spot instead -- max loss = CAD 90,000 premium, capped
Forward (symmetric)Option (asymmetric)
Upfront costNoneCAD 90,000 premium
If rate moves favorablyLocked out of the benefitKeeps the benefit (minus premium)
If rate moves unfavorablyFully protected, dollar-for-dollarFully protected, capped loss = premium
Worst case (hindsight)Gave up upside for nothingLost the premium for nothing
Takeaway. Neither choice is objectively better — a Forward is cheaper and simpler when fully certain about the future need; an Option costs more but is right when the hedger also wants to preserve upside. The same trade-off recurs identically in Section 8's Equity example.

07 · Proxy hedging

Worked Example — Commodity Hedging & Basis Risk (Imperfect Hedges)

Reusing Module 7 §4d's Commodity Option — the clearest illustration that a hedge doesn't need to reference the exact thing being hedged, but that shortcut carries basis risk.

Mismatched UnderlyingJet Fuel via WTI
THE ACTUAL EXPOSURE: an airline (Bank Of Toronto's client) is
exposed to JET FUEL prices rising -- its real operating cost.

THE HEDGE INSTRUMENT: a WTI Crude Oil Call option (Module 7 §4d)
  Strike USD 80.00/bbl | Notional 100,000 bbl | Premium USD 3.20/bbl
  Premium Paid = USD 320,000

WHY WTI, NOT JET FUEL? No sufficiently liquid, exchange-listed
jet fuel derivative exists -- WTI crude is a PROXY, highly
correlated (a major input to refining jet fuel) but NOT identical.

THE GAP -- BASIS RISK:
  WTI +10% and jet fuel +10% -> hedge offsets the cost rise well.

  WTI +10% but jet fuel only +4% (a jet-fuel-specific shock,
  unrelated to crude) -- the hedge OVER-corrects.

  Jet fuel rises MORE than WTI (widening "crack spread") -- the
  hedge UNDER-corrects, leaving the airline still exposed.
Takeaway. Contrast with Section 5's CDS, which references the exact same reference entity — here the hedge instrument and the exposure are related but distinct risk factors, so some risk always survives. Basis risk isn't a flaw; it's the cost of hedging with the most liquid available instrument instead of a bespoke one that may not exist.

08 · Completing the set

Worked Example — Equity Hedging (Protective Put)

A new worked example, built on Module 4 §4a's existing KO position, completing the asset-class set with the same asymmetric-hedge shape Section 6 showed for FX.

Protective PutMaple Leaf Pension Fund · KO
EXISTING POSITION: 10,000 shares KO, bought @ USD 68.40
                    (ISIN US1912161007, Module 4 §4a)

Ahead of an uncertain earnings announcement, Maple Leaf Pension
Fund BUYS a protective put instead of selling outright -- keeps
the position (and upside) but caps downside:

Trade: KO Put, Strike USD 65.00, Notional 10,000 shares, Expiry
       1 month
Premium: USD 1.20/share -> Premium Paid = 10,000 x 1.20 = USD 12,000

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario A: KO falls to USD 58.00 (bad earnings)
  Put in-the-money: EXERCISE, sell at 65.00 instead of market
  58.00 -- avoided loss = (65.00 - 58.00) x 10,000 = USD 70,000
  -> the floor is fixed regardless of how far below 65.00 KO falls

AT EXPIRY -- Scenario B: KO rises to USD 75.00 (good earnings)
  Put expires WORTHLESS -- selling at market beats exercising
  Maple Leaf Pension Fund keeps the FULL upside, out only the
  USD 12,000 premium -- exactly Module 6 §4d's FX Option pattern
Takeaway. A protective put is economically identical in shape to Section 6's FX Option hedge — pay a premium, keep the upside, cap the downside — applied to equity instead of FX. Maple Leaf Pension Fund could instead have sold E-mini futures (symmetric, no premium, but giving up upside too) — the same symmetric-vs-asymmetric choice applies across asset classes.

09 · A legal/accounting label

Hedge Accounting & the Regulatory Angle — "Hedge" Is Not Just a Trading Strategy

Under IFRS 9/ASC 815 and under EMIR/Dodd-Frank, a genuinely risk-reducing trade can qualify for materially different treatment — but only if formally documented as a hedge at inception.

Consequence of hedge statusWhat changesWhy it matters operationally
Hedge accounting (IFRS 9 / ASC 815)Hedge instrument's gain/loss recognized in the same period as the hedged item's offsetting loss/gainWithout it, a risk-reducing trade can make reported earnings look MORE volatile
EMIR clearing exemptionNon-financial counterparty hedging a genuine commercial risk counts differently against the NFC+/NFC- thresholdDetermines mandatory central clearing obligation
Dodd-Frank end-user exceptionUS non-financial end-user hedging real commercial risk exempt from mandatory clearingSame purpose, different jurisdiction
Callout — a simplified illustration, not the literal current rule text. Exact effectiveness-testing thresholds, NFC+/NFC- calculations, and end-user exception criteria are technical, jurisdiction-specific, and revised periodically — the same hedge this curriculum's other reference modules apply to their own regulatory specifics.
Why This Matters for a Booking System's Data Model
A trade being ECONOMICALLY a hedge (P&L moves opposite to another
position) is NOT the same fact as being FORMALLY DESIGNATED a
hedge for accounting/regulatory purposes -- the second requires:

  -> a "Hedge Designation" flag on the trade record, set AT
     INCEPTION (not retroactively)
  -> a documented link to the SPECIFIC hedged item/exposure
  -> ongoing EFFECTIVENESS TESTING data

Without all three captured, an economically sound hedge (like
Section 4's futures trade) still gets treated as a plain
speculative position by accounting and, potentially, by the
clearing-obligation rules.
Takeaway. "Hedge" carries real legal and accounting weight — a well-designed trade capture system needs an explicit hedge-designation data model sitting alongside the trade's plain economic terms, because the same instrument can be a hedge or speculation depending on facts the trade record must capture, not infer.

Q1 — Whether the trader already holds an existing exposure the new trade offsets; the instrument itself never reveals which one it is

Section 1's side-by-side example: Bank Of Toronto's short T-Note futures (against owned bonds) vs. Maple Leaf Pension Fund's long E-mini futures (no offsetting position) — same contract type, opposite motivations.

Q2 — Some residual movement almost always remains (basis risk, imperfect hedge-ratio sizing, counterparty/wrong-way risk), so the combined P&L is flatter, not flat

Section 2's takeaway states this directly — hedging narrows the range of outcomes, it doesn't erase it.

Q3 — Choosing the Forward gives up any chance of a favorable move (locked in either way, no premium); choosing the Option costs an upfront premium in exchange for keeping the upside
Section 6's worked forward-vs-option comparison states this with real numbers on both sides.
Q4 — A bond and a bond future don't have identical price sensitivity to a rate move; the correct approach matches DV01 (risk sensitivity), not face value

Section 4's hedge-ratio formula: contracts needed ~ exposure's DV01 ÷ future's DV01 per contract.

Q5 — The CDS references the exact same reference entity as the bond, unlike a proxy hedge; it still carries wrong-way risk if the protection seller is more likely to fail exactly when the reference entity does

Section 5's takeaway cross-references Module 2 §9's own wrong-way-risk flag.

Q6 — Basis risk is the residual exposure surviving a hedge because the hedge instrument's price driver is related to, but not identical to, the actual exposure's driver (WTI vs. jet fuel); the CDS mostly avoids this by referencing the identical entity

Q7 — Both pay a premium upfront for a capped, known maximum loss if the market moves unfavorably, while keeping the full benefit if it moves favorably

Q8 — Hedge accounting and clearing exemptions require FORMAL documentation captured at inception (designation, linked hedged item, effectiveness testing) — not just that the P&L happens to move opposite to another position
