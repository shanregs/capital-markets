01 · Standardized, and cleared the instant it trades

What Is a Listed Derivative? — Theory & Terminology

A Listed Derivative is a Future or Option that trades on a regulated exchange, with standardized contract terms, and clears through that exchange's own CCP from the moment of execution. This module already showed up piecemeal — Module 4 §4c's E-mini S&P 500 future, Module 4 §4d's AAPL listed call, Module 7 §4b's WTI crude future — each flagged as belonging here, not to their underlying's asset-class module.

TermMeaning
Contract SpecificationThe exchange-defined, standardized terms of one contract — size, quality/grade, expiry cycle, tick size — identical for every trade in that contract
Tick Size / Tick ValueThe smallest allowed price increment, and its cash value — e.g. Module 4 §4c's E-mini moves in 0.25-point ticks worth USD 12.50 each
Contract Month CodeA single-letter code for the delivery/expiry month (F=Jan, G=Feb, H=Mar, J=Apr, K=May, M=Jun, N=Jul, Q=Aug, U=Sep, V=Oct, X=Nov, Z=Dec) — embedded in the exchange contract code (Section 06)
Open InterestThe total number of contracts currently outstanding — a liquidity metric with no OTC equivalent
Novation at ExecutionThe CCP becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer the INSTANT a trade executes (Module 3 §6, Module 7 §5)
Takeaway. Every defining feature of a Listed Derivative exists for exactly one reason: to make the contract fungible, so any buyer's position can net against any seller's position on the exchange's books — precisely what an OTC-negotiated bespoke contract can never do.

03 · Standardization is a venue property

What Exactly Is Being Traded? — A Contract Referencing Anything, Standardized For Fungibility

Unlike an OTC contract (Module 6 §3's FX, Module 8 §3's Rates), a Listed Derivative's terms are fixed by the exchange, not negotiated between two counterparties. Two traders executing the same contract an hour apart get identical terms, down to the tick.

Diagram · OTC vs ListedNegotiated vs standardized
OTC FRA (Module 8 §4b):
  Bank Of Toronto and Goldman Sachs negotiate a bespoke Notional
  (CAD 20,000,000), a bespoke FRA Rate (4.85%), a bespoke period
  (3x6) -- no two FRAs need look alike.

Listed Future (this module):
  Every E-mini S&P 500 future traded on CME today has the SAME
  multiplier (USD 50/point), the SAME tick size (0.25 points),
  and expires on ONE of a small set of standardized quarterly
  dates -- the only thing that varies trade-to-trade is price
  and quantity.

-> This is WHY a Listed Derivative can be netted, closed out, or
   rolled (Module 7 §4b) against ANY other market participant's
   opposite position in the same contract -- an OTC trade can
   only ever be unwound against the SAME original counterparty
   (or novated via a separate compression exercise), because no
   two OTC trades are guaranteed to share identical terms.
Takeaway. This is the real answer to "why does this taxonomy split Futures and Listed Options out from Equity/Commodities/Rates/FX instead of leaving them where the underlying naturally sits?" — standardization is a venue property, not an asset class property, so a schema organized around "how is this contract structured and cleared" genuinely needs a different axis than a schema organized around "what does its value depend on."

NEXT BUSINESS DAY, price falls to 111'24 (111.750) — rates rose,

05 · Delivery, but the short chooses

Trade Lifecycle — What's Genuinely New: Physical Delivery via a Basket

Module 4 §5 and Module 7 §5 already walked through the Futures lifecycle in full. What's genuinely new here is what happens at expiry for a bond future specifically.

Diagram · What Happens At ExpiryThree futures, three delivery mechanics
E-mini S&P 500 Future (Module 4 §4c) at expiry:
  CASH-SETTLED against the index level -- no security moves

WTI Crude Future (Module 7 §4b) at expiry:
  PHYSICALLY SETTLED into ONE specific grade/location of crude
  (if not rolled -- almost never allowed to reach this point)

10-Year T-Note Future (this section) at expiry:
  PHYSICALLY SETTLED, but into ANY ELIGIBLE BOND from a defined
  DELIVERY BASKET (a range of Treasury Notes with maturities in
  a specified window) -- the SHORT (Bank Of Toronto, Section 04c)
  CHOOSES which eligible bond to deliver, using a published
  CONVERSION FACTOR to adjust for that bond's specific coupon
  and maturity versus the contract's notional 6% coupon

-> The bond the short chooses to deliver is called the CHEAPEST
   TO DELIVER (CTD) -- the eligible bond that's most economical
   for the short to source and deliver, a genuinely unique
   mechanic that neither an index future (no delivery at all)
   nor a commodity future (one fixed grade) has an equivalent of.
Takeaway. "Physically settled" doesn't mean the same thing across every Future in this module — an index future never delivers anything, a commodity future delivers one specific thing, and a bond future delivers the short's choice from a defined menu. A reporting or settlement system that assumes physical settlement always means one predetermined instrument moves will handle a bond future's delivery notice incorrectly.

06 · The first module where UPI/UTI don't apply

Identifiers — Exchange Contract Codes, No Bilateral UTI

Listed Derivatives is the first product family where the UPI/UTI pair that's appeared in every OTC module since Module 2 §12 doesn't apply at all — because it exists specifically for bilaterally negotiated OTC derivatives, and a Listed Derivative was never bilaterally negotiated in the first place.

IdentifierApplies to Listed Derivatives?Example
ISIN / CUSIP / TickerNo — not securities (Section 03)—
Exchange Contract CodeYes — the primary identifier, encoding product + expiry month + yearESZ26 (E-mini S&P 500, Dec 2026); CLZ26 (WTI Crude, Dec 2026 — Module 7 §6); ZNZ26 (10Y T-Note, Dec 2026)
Contract Month CodeEmbedded inside the exchange contract code (Section 01's F/G/H/.../Z table)Z = December, in all three codes above
UPI / UTINo — these identify bilaterally negotiated OTC derivatives (Module 2 §12); a Listed Derivative's fungibility (Section 03) makes per-trade bilateral identifiers meaningless—
LEIYes — identifies Bank Of Toronto and any other counterparty, same role as every prior moduleEntity, not instrument (Module 4 §6)
Takeaway. A Listed Derivative trade record with an exchange contract code but no UPI/UTI is exactly correct — the reverse of the diagnostic Module 6 §6 and Module 7 §6 already established for OTC records. Seeing a UPI/UTI populated on what's otherwise clearly a listed Futures/Options record would itself be the anomaly here.

07 · One cell, not a grid

Exchange vs OTC vs Bilateral vs Centrally Cleared

Every other module turned this section into a genuine two-axis question with multiple answers. Listed Derivatives is the one node where the question has a single, unconditional answer for every product in it — by definition.

ProductExchange or OTC?Bilateral or Centrally Cleared?
Futures (any underlying)Always exchange-tradedAlways centrally cleared, from the instant of execution (Section 01)
Listed Options (any underlying)Always exchange-tradedAlways centrally cleared (OCC for US equity options, CME Clearing for futures options)
Takeaway. Every prior module's Section 07 existed to show WHERE a given product sits on this two-axis grid, because the answer genuinely varied by product. Here, the grid collapses to one cell — which is the whole point of this module existing as a separate node: "Listed Derivatives" isn't defined by what it references, it's defined by always landing in the exchange-traded, centrally-cleared cell, no matter which of the five underlyings from Section 02 it's built on.

08 · Where EMIR's OTC machinery mostly doesn't need to reach

Regulatory Perspective — MiFID, EMIR, SFTR, FCA, CIRO, HKMA, DTCC, DMO

The regulatory story here is almost the inverse of every prior module's.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Listed Derivatives specifically
MiFID II / MiFIR (EU)Sets pre/post-trade transparency for listed derivatives trading venues directly, plus position limits on certain listed commodity derivatives (Module 7 §08) — reused here since the mechanism is the same regardless of underlying
EMIRGenerally does not apply the way it does to OTC derivatives (Module 2 §07) — the reporting obligation still technically exists, but the mandatory clearing half of EMIR (headline in Module 8 §08 and Module 9 §08) is moot here, since these products were never going to be anything BUT centrally cleared

Takeaway. Most of Section 08's rows here are really saying "this regulation's OTC-focused machinery mostly doesn't need to reach into this corner of the market, because Section 07 already guarantees central clearing without a mandate." The CFTC/SEC Security Futures Product split is the one genuine exception — regulatory attention specifically increases because a listed derivative's underlying happens to be a single security.

09 · One venue, one CCP

Canadian Systems Landscape — Front, Middle & Back Office to Regulatory Reporting

systems-landscape §02 already named the generic Front/Middle/Back Office architecture for Canada. This section specializes that architecture to Listed Derivatives specifically — because every underlying (Section 02) funnels through the same mechanic, Canada's listed-derivatives stack is unusually simple to draw: there is genuinely only ONE venue and ONE CCP for exchange-listed derivatives in Canada, unlike the several competing venues cash equities use.

Front OfficeOrder/Execution Management (Bloomberg EMSX, FlexTrade, or a bank's own DMA gateway — illustrative) → Montreal Exchange (MX), TMX Group's derivatives exchange, matched on its electronic trading engine, commonly referred to as SOLAOrder entry, execution, price discovery

Back Office / CCPCDCC (Canadian Derivatives Clearing Corporation), a TMX Group companyNovates every MX-listed trade at execution (Section 01), collects Initial Margin, calculates daily Variation Margin (SPAN-style — illustrative)
Cash SettlementLVTS (Large Value Transfer System) — systems-landscape §08's cash-rail table, Canada's counterpart to Fedwire/CHAPSMoves variation-margin and settlement cash between Bank Of Toronto's settlement bank and CDCC
Regulatory / OversightCIRO (dealer conduct); the CSA (recognizes MX/CDCC); the Bank of Canada (oversees CDCC as a systemically important FMI, alongside CDS/CDSX)Conduct oversight and systemic-risk monitoring — not a Trade Repository submission (see takeaway)

Callout — illustrative, not prescriptive. System/vendor names above (Bloomberg EMSX, FlexTrade, Murex, Calypso) are representative of what Canadian bank derivatives desks commonly run, not a claim about Bank Of Toronto's actual internal technology stack — consistent with how systems-landscape §02 already hedges its own vendor list. MX, CDCC, CIRO, the CSA, the Bank of Canada, and LVTS, by contrast, are real, specific Canadian market infrastructure: there is only ONE CCP for MX-listed products, not a choice of vendors, unlike the front/middle-office layer above it.
Takeaway. Section 08 already flagged that EMIR's mandatory-clearing machinery is "moot" for Listed Derivatives because central clearing was never optional here — this section shows the Canadian-specific consequence of that same fact: because every MX-listed trade is centrally cleared through CDCC from execution, there's no CSA Multilateral Instrument 96-101 Trade Repository submission the way an uncleared CAD IRS (Module 8) or a CDS (Module 9) would require. CIRO's own market/trade surveillance — not a Trade Repository — is the oversight layer that answers Module 14 §1's "did this trade get seen by a regulator?" question for this corner of the Canadian market.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Variation Margin = USD 75,000, and Bank Of Toronto LOSES (pays out), the mirror image of Section 04c's worked example
Math · Price Rise, Short Position10Y T-Note Future
Variation Margin = (113.250 - 112.500)/100 x 100,000 x 100
                  = 0.0075 x 100,000 x 100
                  = USD 75,000

Since Bank Of Toronto is SHORT (sold the future), a price RISE moves against the position — Bank Of Toronto now owes USD 75,000 in variation margin, the opposite of Section 04c's price-fall scenario where the short gained. This is the hedge working as intended: if rates fell instead of rose, Bank Of Toronto's actual bond portfolio would have GAINED value, offsetting this futures loss — the whole reason it sold the future in the first place.

Q2 — An OTC FRA's terms are bespoke to one negotiation; a Listed Future's terms are identical for every trade in that contract

Section 03's takeaway states this as the core reason standardization exists at all: fungibility is what makes closing out or rolling (Module 7 §4b) possible against a completely different counterparty than the one you originally traded with — an OTC FRA has no fungible "opposite position" to net against except the original counterparty.

Q3 — No — the SELLER (the short) chooses which eligible bond from the delivery basket to deliver, using the conversion factor

Section 05 states this directly, contrasting it with both the E-mini future (no delivery at all) and the WTI future (one fixed grade/location) — the bond future is the only one of the three where the party making delivery has a genuine choice of what to deliver (the "Cheapest to Deliver," or CTD).

Q4 — No, you would NOT expect a UTI, because Section 06 establishes UPI/UTI only apply to bilaterally negotiated OTC derivatives, and a Listed Future was never bilaterally negotiated

This directly contrasts with the OTC Commodity Swap (Module 7 §6), which DOES carry a UPI/UTI precisely because it IS bilaterally negotiated — the exact same underlying commodity (crude oil) produces two structurally different identifier patterns purely based on which venue/product type it's traded through.

Q5 — Every product in this module lands in the SAME cell (exchange-traded, centrally cleared) by definition, unlike every prior module's grid with multiple populated cells
Every prior module needed Section 07 to show WHERE within a grid each of that asset class's several products landed. Here, there's nothing to differentiate — which is exactly the point Section 07's takeaway makes about why "Listed Derivatives" earns its own module instead of being an asset-class-organized concept.
Q6 — A Security Futures Product's underlying IS a single security, a direct securities-law hook; Credit's split was about swaps-vs-security-based-swaps classification under Dodd-Frank's OTC framework

Section 08 flags this explicitly as "a second CFTC/SEC split... reached for a different reason" — both splits trace back to the same root cause (a single security's involvement triggering SEC interest) but apply to genuinely different product categories (OTC swaps vs. listed futures/options).

Q7 — Standardization/central clearing is a VENUE property, not an ASSET CLASS property — a Future or Listed Option can reference any of the six asset classes' underlyings

Folding Listed Derivatives into just one asset class module (say, Equity) would misrepresent every Future/Listed Option built on a different underlying (a WTI future, a T-Note future) as if it somehow belonged to Equity instead. The work data model taxonomy's own structure (Listed Derivatives as node "7," separate from asset-class nodes "1" through "6") reflects a real organizing principle, not an arbitrary schema-design choice.

Q8 — Because the SXF future is centrally cleared through CDCC from the instant of execution — CDCC itself already holds the complete, centralized record of every position, so MI 96-101's visibility gap doesn't exist here. CIRO's own market/trade surveillance provides the regulatory oversight instead of a Trade Repository
Section 09's takeaway states this directly, tying back to Section 08's observation that EMIR's mandatory-clearing machinery is "moot" here — the same logic applies to Canada's own OTC trade-reporting regime: MI 96-101 targets exactly the visibility gap that central clearing through a single national CCP (CDCC) already closes for every MX-listed product. An uncleared CAD IRS has no such centralized record, which is exactly the gap MI 96-101 exists to close instead.
