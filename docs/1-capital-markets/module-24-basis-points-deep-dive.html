01 · The unit itself

What Is a Basis Point? — Definition & Why Percent Isn't Precise Enough

A basis point (bp, plural bps) is one hundredth of one percent — 0.01%, or 0.0001 in decimal form. It's the standard unit capital markets uses whenever a percentage move is too small, or too consequential, to describe usefully in whole percentage points.

The Conversion Every Other Section Assumes
1 basis point (1 bp)   = 0.01%   = 0.0001
10 basis points         = 0.10%   = 0.0010
25 basis points         = 0.25%   = 0.0025
100 basis points        = 1.00%   = 0.0100
250 basis points        = 2.50%   = 0.0250

-> "bps" is always ADDITIVE on a percentage/rate scale, never
   MULTIPLICATIVE -- a rate moving from 4.00% to 4.25% moved
   "up 25bps," not "up 6.25%" (the PERCENTAGE CHANGE of 4.00
   relative to itself -- a different, less useful number here).

Why not just say "0.25%"? Two reasons this curriculum's other modules already lean on:

Precision without clutter. A rate move from 4.00% to 4.03% is "0.03%" in percent — awkward, easy to misread as 3%. "3 basis points" is unambiguous at a glance.

A common unit across wildly different base numbers. A 25bp move means the same thing — 0.0025 added to a rate — whether the starting rate is 0.50% or 12.00%. Bps are an absolute, not relative, unit of rate change.

Takeaway. Every worked example in this module — Fixed Income, Rates, Credit, Equity, FX — reduces to the same arithmetic fact: bps × 0.0001 × exposure = dollar impact, just with a different "exposure" plugged in each time (DV01, Spread DV01, notional, fee base). Section 2 maps exactly where each asset class plugs bps in.

02 · Six asset classes, different uses

Basis Points Across Asset Classes — Where They Show Up

Every asset class in this curriculum's roadmap uses bps somewhere, but for genuinely different purposes. Confusing which use is in play is a common source of reporting-system errors.

Asset classWhere bps show upWhat moves per bpExample
Fixed IncomeBond yield (YTM) & price sensitivityPrice, via DV01Section 3
RatesThe floating/fixed rate itself, swap valuationSwap MTM, via Swap DV01Section 4
CreditCDS spread, bond credit spreadProtection value, via Spread DV01Section 5
EquityManagement/advisory fees, bid-ask spreadsFee cost, execution costSection 6
FXRarely used directly — quoted in pips insteadExchange rate, in pip termsSection 7
CommoditiesLess common directly; tick sizes usually in price units—See Module 7
Takeaway. The single unifying thread: bps measure a rate, yield, spread, or fee — never a raw price. FX mostly opts out in favor of pips (Section 7); Equity's use is narrower (fees, trading costs, not the stock price itself, Section 6). A system that tags a raw equity price move "up 150bps" is almost certainly using the term loosely, not precisely.

03 · The cleanest bps-to-dollars translation

Worked Example — Fixed Income: DV01 & Price Impact From a Yield Move

Reusing Module 5 §4a's Canada 5% Bond 2035 position and Module 10 §3's DV01 math — a bond's price and yield move in lockstep, one basis point at a time.

DV01 — Canada 5% Bond 2035Module 5 §4a / Module 10 §3
Modified Duration: 7.2 | Market Value: CAD 9,850,000

DV01 = Modified Duration x Market Value x 0.0001
     = 7.2 x 9,850,000 x 0.0001
     = CAD 7,092 per basis point

SCENARIO A -- yields RISE 25 basis points:
  Approx. Price Impact = -DV01 x Basis Points Moved
                        = -7,092 x 25
                        = -CAD 177,300  (bond LOSES value)

SCENARIO B -- yields FALL 40 basis points:
  Approx. Price Impact = +7,092 x 40 = +CAD 283,680 (bond GAINS value)

-> Bond prices and yields move INVERSELY (Module 1 §1) -- DV01
   is symmetric in size but the SIGN flips depending on direction.

Why YTM itself is quoted to the basis point. A bond's Yield to Maturity (Module 5 §1, Module 10 §3) is conventionally quoted to two decimal places as a percentage — this bond's own YTM, 5.21% (Module 10 §3), is exactly "521 basis points" said differently. Every DV01 calculation measures the price impact of that YTM ticking by single bps, which is why DV01 is defined "per basis point" rather than "per percent."

Takeaway. DV01 (Dollar Value of 01) is Fixed Income's core bps-to-dollars converter, plugging directly into valuation (what is this bond worth right now) and MTM (how much did that worth change, expressed as bps moved × DV01). Section 9 generalizes this exact mechanic across every other asset class.

04 · Same mechanic, a swap's cash flows

Worked Example — Rates: Swap DV01 & MTM on an Interest Rate Swap

Reusing Module 8 §4a's 5Y CAD IRS and Module 10 §4a's MTM math — the same DV01 concept as Section 3, computed off a swap's cash flow schedule instead of a bond's.

Swap DV01 & MTM — 5Y CAD IRSModule 8 §4a / Module 10 §4a
Trade ID BOT-IRS-2026-034410
Bank Of Toronto: pays FIXED 4.00%, receives FLOATING (CORRA)
Notional (reference only, never exchanged): CAD 100,000,000

Swap DV01 (approx., 4.5Y remaining): ~CAD 41,000 per basis point

RATE MOVES from 4.00% to 4.30% (a 30bp RISE in the swap curve):
  Approx. MTM Gain to Bank Of Toronto = 41,000 x 30
                                       = CAD 1,230,000

-> Bank Of Toronto pays a FIXED rate now BELOW the market rate for
   an equivalent new swap -- its position is worth MORE. The
   fixed-rate PAYER gains when rates RISE; the RECEIVER loses the
   same CAD 1,230,000.

Why the same DV01 concept, a different number. Section 3's bond DV01 (CAD 7,092/bp) and this swap's DV01 (CAD 41,000/bp) aren't comparable in isolation — the swap's Notional is roughly 10x the bond's Market Value, but its DV01 is roughly 5.8x, because a swap's DV01 also depends on the shape of its remaining cash flow schedule, not Notional alone. DV01 is never inferred from Notional by a rule of thumb — it's the output of a full revaluation.

Takeaway. A swap's MTM is, in practice, almost always reported as "bps moved × Swap DV01" between full revaluations — the same arithmetic shape as Section 3's bond, confirming Section 1's central formula, just with Swap DV01 standing in for bond DV01.

05 · Bps quote the risk premium itself

Worked Example — Credit: CDS Spread in Basis Points & Spread DV01

Reusing Module 9 §4a-b's 5Y CDS and Module 10 §4c's spread-driven MTM math — the asset class where basis points quote the risk premium itself, not a benchmark rate.

CDS Spread in BpsModule 9 §4a-b / Module 10 §4c
AT INCEPTION:
  Notional: USD 10,000,000
  Running Coupon: 100 bps (fixed, contractual, quarterly)
  Market Spread at inception: 250 bps (market-judged credit risk)

  Because coupon (100bps) is below market spread (250bps) at trade
  date, Bank Of Toronto pays an upfront:
    Upfront ~ (2.50% - 1.00%) x ~4.5 (Risky Duration) x 10,000,000
            = USD 675,000

ONE YEAR LATER -- market spread WIDENS to 400 bps, Risky Duration ~4.0:
  Current Approx. Value = (Market Spread - Running Coupon)
                            x Risky Duration x Notional
                         = (4.00% - 1.00%) x 4.0 x 10,000,000
                         = USD 1,200,000

  Unrealized Gain to Bank Of Toronto (protection buyer)
    = 1,200,000 - 675,000 = USD 525,000

-> "Spread DV01" (CS01) is the DV01 concept applied to a 1bp move
   in the CREDIT SPREAD instead of a benchmark rate.

Why credit spreads move faster/wider than rates. A benchmark rate typically moves in 25bp increments at scheduled central bank meetings (Section 8). A credit spread has no such schedule — it can widen or narrow by dozens or hundreds of bps within days on company-specific news, because it prices a single entity's default risk, not a shared policy rate.

Takeaway. A CDS's Running Coupon is fixed at inception; its market value changes because the market spread — quoted in bps, moving continuously — diverges from that fixed coupon. Same "locked in vs. what the market offers today" mechanic as Sections 3–4, with Spread DV01 replacing bond/swap DV01.

06 · Cost, not price

Worked Example — Equity: Basis Points in Fees & Trading Costs

Equity is the clearest illustration that bps don't automatically apply to a raw price. Where they genuinely show up is cost — management fees and bid-ask spreads, built on Module 4's Maple Leaf Pension Fund positions.

Management Fee in BpsIllustrative, Module 4 positions
Maple Leaf Pension Fund equity allocation: CAD 500,000,000
(aggregate, including the 10,000-share KO holding, Module 4 §4a)

Annual Management Fee: 45 basis points (0.45%) of AUM -- an "MER"
in fund-industry terms

Annual Fee Cost = 500,000,000 x 0.0045 = CAD 2,250,000

-> Same "bps x 0.0001 x exposure" formula -- here "exposure" is
   Assets Under Management, and the bps figure is a FEE RATE, not
   a market-observed yield or spread.
Bid-Ask Spread in Bps — AAPL
AAPL Bid: USD 259.98 | Ask: USD 260.02
Absolute Spread = 260.02 - 259.98 = USD 0.04

Spread in bps = (Absolute Spread / Mid Price) x 10,000
              = (0.04 / 260.00) x 10,000
              = 1.5 bps

-> Quoting in bps lets a desk compare execution cost ACROSS stocks
   with different price levels on a common scale.

Why the stock price itself usually isn't quoted in bps. AAPL moving from USD 260.00 to USD 262.60 is a 1.00% move — called "up a percent" or "up 260 points" far more often than "up 100bps," even though the arithmetic is identical. A bond yield, swap rate, or CDS spread is already percentage-shaped before any move happens; a stock price is a dollar figure, so bps there are a deliberate choice, not the default.

Takeaway. Equity draws the sharpest line in this module between "bps describes a rate/yield/spread/fee" and "bps does NOT automatically describe a raw price" — a distinction a reporting system needs to get right to avoid confusion with the yield- and spread-based sensitivities every other asset class here uses bps for.

07 · A deliberately different unit

Worked Example — FX: Basis Points vs. Pips

Reusing Module 6 §4a's USD/CAD Spot trade to introduce pips — the unit FX actually quotes moves in, related to bps but not the same thing.

Pips vs. Basis Points — USD/CADModule 6 §4a
USD/CAD Spot Rate: 1.3500 (quoted to 4 decimal places)
1 PIP = smallest standard quoted move = 0.0001 -- the LAST decimal
        place of the quoted rate

Rate moves from 1.3500 to 1.3520 -- a move of 0.0020 = 20 PIPS

IS THIS "20 BASIS POINTS"? -- Only by coincidence, and approximately:
  20 pips / 1.3500 x 10,000 = 14.8 bps of the RATE ITSELF

-> A pip is a FIXED decimal increment; a basis point is 0.01% of
   whatever number it's attached to. For USD/JPY (2 decimals),
   1 pip = 0.01 -- a much bigger fraction of a ~150.00 rate.

Why FX doesn't just use bps. Unlike a bond yield or swap rate, an FX spot rate isn't inherently a percentage — it's a ratio of two currencies, with a decimal convention that varies by pair. Pips give a fixed, pair-specific tick size, independent of the percentage that tick happens to represent. Where FX genuinely does use bps directly is FX Forward points and cross-currency basis — both rate-shaped numbers, not spot-rate ticks.

Takeaway. Treating pips and bps as interchangeable is a common, usually-harmless-for-USD/CAD simplification that breaks visibly for other pairs — a well-designed FX system should track pips natively for spot/forward moves, and compute a "bps of rate" figure only when that's genuinely what's being asked for.

08 · Two different clocks

How Often Do Basis Points Actually Change? — Scheduled Policy vs. Continuous Pricing

"Basis points" describes a unit, not a frequency — but the things measured in bps change on very different clocks depending on which one it is.

What movesTypical frequencyTypical size per moveExample
Central bank policy rateScheduled meetings, ~8/year25bp norm; 50/75bp in fast cycles; 0bp holds commonSection 4's curve moves
Bond yields / YTMContinuously, every sessionSingle-digit bps quiet; tens of bps volatileSection 3
Swap ratesContinuouslySimilar to bond yieldsSection 4
Credit spreadsContinuously, sharp company-specific jumps100+ bps in a day on newsSection 5
Management feesRarely — contractual, annual reviewsN/A — not market-drivenSection 6
Bid-ask spreadsContinuously, with liquidityCan double/triple in stressSection 6
Collateral/margin haircutsPeriodic reviews, not continuousOften expressed in bpsModule 16 — SFTR
Two Genuinely Different "How Often" Questions
"How often does the POLICY RATE change?"
  -> ~8 scheduled opportunities a year per major central bank, and
     it can simply NOT move. A DECISION, not a continuously
     observed price.

"How often does a bond's YIELD / a swap's RATE / a CDS's SPREAD
change?"
  -> Continuously, every trade -- OBSERVED MARKET PRICES reacting
     to everything, including but not limited to expectations
     about the next policy decision.
Takeaway. A policy rate changing 25bps eight times a year and a bond's YTM ticking by fractions of a bp every few seconds are both "basis point moves," but answer entirely different questions — one a scheduled decision, the other continuous market price discovery. A "how stale is this rate" check needs to know which kind it's holding.

09 · The mechanism, tied together

Basis Points' Impact on Valuation, MTM & YTM

Does a bps move actually change what a position is worth, what its MTM shows, and what its YTM reads? Yes — directly and mechanically, via a "per-basis-point sensitivity" that's structurally identical across asset classes.

ConceptWhat it answersThe bps mechanism
ValuationWhat is this position worth right now?Current Value = f(current YTM/swap rate/spread) — full revaluation, to the basis point
MTMHow much did that worth change?MTM Change ≈ Basis Points Moved × [DV01/Swap DV01/Spread DV01]
YTMWhat am I earning if held to maturity?Quoted to the basis point (Section 3's bond: 5.21% = 521bps); a change in market YTM drives the price impact DV01 measures
The Same Shape, Three Times Over
FIXED INCOME:  Price Impact  = -DV01        x Basis Points Moved
RATES (SWAP):  MTM Change    =  Swap DV01   x Basis Points Moved
CREDIT (CDS):  Value Change  =  Spread DV01 x Basis Points Moved

-> Every one of these is "exposure's per-basis-point sensitivity,
   times how many basis points actually moved." Only the sign
   convention and which sensitivity measure applies differ -- the
   arithmetic shape is identical.

One important nuance. A bond's Coupon Rate is fixed at issuance; YTM moves purely because market Price moves relative to that fixed coupon. A 25bp YTM increase reflects the market re-pricing the same fixed coupon stream at a less generous price — YTM is a derived, market-driven number recomputed continuously off Price, never a contractual term set the way a Coupon Rate is.

Takeaway. Basis points aren't a side detail attached to valuation, MTM, and YTM — they're the unit these three concepts are measured and moved in, and the DV01-family sensitivities are the mechanical bridge between "the market moved by this many bps" and "my position's dollar value changed by this many dollars."

10 · A genuine data-quality control point

Basis Points in Regulatory, Collateral & Reporting Context

Several fields this curriculum's reporting modules already require are, at their core, bps figures — and getting the unit wrong in a reported field is a real, recurring operational error.

ContextWhere bps appearsCoverage
CDS spread reportingThe "Spread" field on an OTC derivative trade report (EMIR/Dodd-Frank), quoted in bpsModule 9, Module 2 §9
Rate benchmark resetsCORRA/SOFR/€STR/SONIA fixings, spread-over-benchmark legsModule 8 §1
Collateral/margin haircutsHaircut percentages, often adjusted in bpsModule 12, Module 16 — SFTR
Repo ratesThe repo rate and "repo spread" over a benchmark, in bpsRepo/SFT worked example
MiFID II / MiFIR fieldsReported price/yield fields carry bps-level precision requirementsModule 20
Callout — a genuine operational failure mode. A field that should hold "523" meaning 523 basis points (5.23%) being mistakenly populated with "5.23" (read downstream as 5.23 basis points, 0.0523%) is a two-order-of-magnitude error — the kind of unit-of-measure bug Modules 14 and 20 flag as a recurring real-world data-quality issue. A field's documentation should always state explicitly whether it's "in basis points," "in percent," or "in decimal" — never leave it implied.
Takeaway. Basis points show up as literal reported fields, not just desk shorthand — the unit-conversion discipline from Section 1 is a genuine data-quality control point in any trade capture or reporting system touching rate-, spread-, or yield-denominated fields.
