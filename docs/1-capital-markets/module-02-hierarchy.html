01 · From Asset Class down to a single Trade

The Hierarchy — Four Layers

Every record you'll ever see in a reporting system sits at the bottom of a four-layer hierarchy. The top three layers describe what is being traded and never change. The bottom layer describes one specific event and gets a brand-new record every single time.

Takeaway. Asset Class, Product, and Instrument are reference data — look them up once, they don't move. Trade is transactional data — there can be thousands of Trade records all pointing at the exact same Instrument (exactly like the "One Issuance, Many Investors" example in Module 1).

02 · Which exact thing this is

Instrument Identifiers — ISIN vs CUSIP vs Ticker

Three different ways to identify the same kind of thing, with very different scope and coverage.

ISIN

International Securities Identification Number — 12 characters, globally unique: 2-letter country code + a 9-character national security identifier + 1 check digit. The de-facto global standard identifier for a security, used across bonds, equities, and increasingly derivatives.

CUSIP

Committee on Uniform Securities Identification Procedures — 9 characters, North America only (US & Canada). For North American securities, the CUSIP is literally embedded inside the ISIN.

Ticker

A short, human-readable trading symbol used on a specific exchange (e.g. AAPL). It is not globally unique — the same ticker can mean different things on different exchanges — and it's really only meaningful for exchange-traded instruments (mainly equities). Bonds and OTC derivatives don't have tickers.

Takeaway. If a reporting record has only a Ticker and no ISIN/CUSIP, that's a strong signal it's equity data straight from an exchange feed — not a bond or OTC derivative, which will almost always carry an ISIN instead.

03 · What each field on a trade means

Core Trade Fields — Trade ID, Quantity vs Notional, Price, Currency

The transactional fields that get generated fresh on every single trade.

Trade ID

A unique reference generated by the executing firm's trading system the instant a trade is captured. It's the thread that ties one specific transaction together across its entire lifecycle (Module 3).

Quantity

For equities and bonds, the actual amount of the instrument bought or sold (shares, or face value). It physically moves at settlement.

Notional

For derivatives, a reference principal used only to calculate periodic cash flows. It never physically moves (full treatment in Module 1, Section 08).

Price & Currency

Price is what one unit costs, in whatever convention that instrument uses: per share, per 100 face value, or an FX rate. Currency is the currency the trade is denominated and settled in.

Worked example — Bank Of Toronto's blotter, three asset classes, one day

Blotter · 17 August 20263 trades
TRADE 1 — BOND
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000512
  Instrument : Canada 5% Bond 2035  (ISIN CA1358XXXXX)
  Quantity   : CAD 10,000,000 face value
  Price      : 98.50  (per 100 face value)
  Currency   : CAD
  Value      : 10,000,000 x 98.50 / 100 = CAD 9,850,000

TRADE 2 — EQUITY
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000513
  Instrument : Apple Inc. common stock  (Ticker AAPL, ISIN US0378331005)
  Quantity   : 5,000 shares
  Price      : USD 227.15 per share
  Currency   : USD
  Value      : 5,000 x 227.15 = USD 1,135,750

TRADE 3 — OTC DERIVATIVE
  Trade ID   : BOT-2026-000514
  Instrument : 5Y CAD Interest Rate Swap vs Goldman Sachs
  Notional   : CAD 100,000,000   (reference only — NEVER exchanged)
  Price      : Bank Of Toronto pays Fixed 4.00% / receives Floating
  Currency   : CAD
  Value      : no upfront value exchanged — only periodic net cash flows
Takeaway. "Quantity" and "Notional" are not interchangeable, even though some systems reuse the same database column for both. Quantity moves — bonds and shares actually change hands at settlement. Notional never moves — only the cash flows calculated from it do.

06 · Things you could physically hold

Important Asset Classes & Products — Cash Instruments

"Cash" (or "physical") instruments are securities that exist and settle on their own — a bond or a share is a real, deliverable thing the moment it's issued. These are classified using ISIN/CUSIP plus a CFI code (ISO 10962 — Classification of Financial Instruments, a 6-character code baked into most security masters). Cash instruments do not get an ISDA classification — ISDA taxonomy is specifically for derivatives (Section 07).

That's exactly why none of them carry an ISDA classification: ISDA only classifies contracts between two parties, which is what a derivative is (Section 07) — not the underlying tradable thing itself.

07 · Classifying contracts, not things

What Is ISDA, and Why Does a Derivative Need an "ISDA Classification"?

ISDA (International Swaps and Derivatives Association) is the trade body that standardizes two separate things people often conflate.

1. The ISDA Master Agreement

The legal contract template two counterparties sign once, which then governs every OTC derivative trade between them (netting, default, collateral terms). Covered properly in Module 12 (Collateral & Margin).

2. The ISDA Product Taxonomy

A standardized classification scheme for labelling what kind of derivative a trade is. This is the piece relevant to regulatory reporting (Module 14): when Bank Of Toronto reports an OTC derivative trade to a Trade Repository under EMIR, MiFIR, or Dodd-Frank/CFTC rules, one of the mandatory fields on that report is literally "ISDA Taxonomy" — it's how regulators bucket millions of trades from every firm into comparable categories without reading each contract by hand.

The ISDA Product Taxonomy is a four-level hierarchy — notice it's a different hierarchy from Section 01's Asset Class → Product → Instrument → Trade; this one classifies the product itself for regulatory purposes, one level more granular than our "Product" layer:

Diagram · ISDA Product Taxonomy4 levels
ISDA Asset Class   ->   Base Product   ->   Sub-Product   ->   Transaction Type
==================================================================================

Interest Rate      ->   IRSwap         ->   FixedFloat     ->   Swap
Credit              ->   SingleName     ->   Corporate      ->   CreditDefaultSwap
Foreign Exchange   ->   Forward        ->   Deliverable    ->   Forward
Equity              ->   Swap           ->   PriceReturn    ->   Swap
Commodity           ->   Energy         ->   Oil            ->   Swap

There are exactly five official ISDA Asset Classes for derivatives — a much shorter, fixed list compared to the open-ended "Asset Class" values you'd see across cash + derivative products combined:

ISDA Asset ClassPlain-English meaningCommon desk name
Interest RateDerivatives whose cash flows depend on interest rates"Rates"
CreditDerivatives whose payout depends on a default/credit event"Credit"
Foreign ExchangeDerivatives whose payout depends on currency rates"FX"
EquityDerivatives whose payout depends on a stock/index price"Equity Derivatives"

Takeaway. If a reporting record has an "ISDA Taxonomy" or "ISDA Asset Class" field populated, that alone tells you it's an OTC derivative, not a cash bond or cash equity — cash instruments simply don't have this field, because ISDA doesn't classify them (Section 06).

09 · Insurance against default

Worked Example — Credit Default Swap (CDS)

The protection buyer pays a periodic premium (the "spread," quoted in basis points per year) to the protection seller. If the reference entity suffers a defined credit event (default, bankruptcy, restructuring), the protection seller pays the buyer a loss amount — otherwise, the seller collects premium and pays nothing.

A CDS has only two parties — the reference entity isn't one of them. Northern Rail Corp is the subject of this contract, not a party to it: it signs nothing, pays nothing, receives nothing, and typically has no idea the trade exists. The link is purely referential — its credit behavior is the trigger condition the two real counterparties' cash flows depend on, the same way an insurance policy on someone else's house pays out based on that house, without the homeowner being a party to the policy.
Diagram · Three Names, Only Two PartiesBank Of Toronto ↔ Goldman Sachs
Bank Of Toronto  <====  CDS CONTRACT  ====>  Goldman Sachs
(protection buyer)     (bilateral,            (protection seller)
                        just these two)

                  Northern Rail Corp
               (reference entity -- outside the
                contract, referenced BY it, not
                a party TO it)

-> Bank Of Toronto pays Goldman Sachs premium; Goldman Sachs pays
   Bank Of Toronto a loss amount IF Northern Rail Corp defaults.

   contract, ever.

In practice, a protection buyer usually has some other exposure to the reference entity it's hedging — e.g. Bank Of Toronto might hold Northern Rail Corp bonds or have lent it money directly — and uses the CDS to transfer that separate credit risk to Goldman Sachs. Nothing about the CDS contract itself requires that exposure to exist, though; a CDS can equally be bought purely to speculate on Northern Rail Corp's credit quality with no underlying position at all.

Worked Example · Northern Rail Corp CDSNotional CAD 20,000,000
Reference Entity : Northern Rail Corp (a corporate borrower — fictional)
Notional          : CAD 20,000,000  (reference only — never exchanged upfront)
Spread            : 120 bps (1.20% per year)
Maturity          : 5 years, quarterly premium payments
Recovery Rate     : 40% (assumed, if default occurs)

SCENARIO A — No default this quarter (the normal case)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Quarterly premium (Bank Of Toronto pays Goldman Sachs):
    20,000,000 x 1.20% x (91 / 365) = CAD 59,836

  Bank Of Toronto  ---- pays CAD 59,836 ---->  Goldman Sachs
  (repeats every quarter for 5 years = 20 payments, unless default happens first)

SCENARIO B — Northern Rail Corp (the reference entity) defaults during the 5 years
----------------------------------------------------------
  Loss Given Default = Notional x (1 - Recovery Rate)
                      = 20,000,000 x (1 - 0.40)
                      = CAD 12,000,000

  Goldman Sachs  ---- pays CAD 12,000,000 ---->  Bank Of Toronto
  (premium payments then stop — the contract has been triggered)
ISDA classification for this trade: Asset Class = Credit, Base Product = SingleName, Sub-Product = Corporate, Transaction Type = CreditDefaultSwap. This is exactly the four-level label a Trade Repository report for this trade would carry.
Takeaway. Notice the CAD 20,000,000 notional never appears in either cash flow above — it's purely the multiplier for the premium calculation, and the base for the loss-given-default calculation. Whether Bank Of Toronto ever sees CAD 12,000,000 depends entirely on whether Northern Rail Corp defaults; most CDS contracts run their full term paying only the small quarterly premium and never trigger.

10 · Locking in tomorrow's rate today

Worked Example — FX Forward

An FX Forward locks in today an exchange rate for a currency exchange that will actually happen on a future date. Unlike FX Spot (Module 1, settled T+1/T+2), a Forward's value date can be weeks or months out — useful for hedging a known future cash need without being exposed to rate movement between now and then.

Worked Example · 3M USD/CAD ForwardNotional USD 10,000,000
Today's Spot Rate (USD/CAD)     : 1.3500   (1 USD = CAD 1.3500)
3-Month Forward Points          : +0.0045  (driven by CAD-USD interest rate gap)
3-Month Forward Rate            : 1.3545   (1.3500 + 0.0045)

Bank Of Toronto agrees TODAY with Goldman Sachs:
  In 3 months, Bank Of Toronto pays CAD 13,545,000
               and receives USD 10,000,000
  (10,000,000 x 1.3545 = 13,545,000)

-> No cash or currency moves today. Only the RATE is locked in now.

3 MONTHS LATER — value date arrives, two possible outcomes:
--------------------------------------------------------------
  If spot has moved to 1.3700 (USD got MORE expensive):
    Bank Of Toronto still only pays 13,545,000, not 13,700,000
    -> Forward SAVED Bank Of Toronto CAD 155,000 vs. buying at spot then.

  If spot has moved to 1.3400 (USD got CHEAPER):
    Bank Of Toronto still pays 13,545,000, even though spot would have
    only cost 13,400,000
    -> Forward COST Bank Of Toronto CAD 145,000 vs. buying at spot then.
       This is not "the trade going wrong" — it's the price of certainty:
       Bank Of Toronto hedged a known future need and gave up any chance
       of a better rate in exchange for eliminating the risk of a worse one.
ISDA classification for this trade: Asset Class = Foreign Exchange, Base Product = Forward, Sub-Product = Deliverable (both currencies physically exchange — contrast with an NDF, where only a cash difference settles because one currency isn't freely convertible), Transaction Type = Forward.
Takeaway. A Forward's Notional (USD 10,000,000 here) does eventually settle, unlike an Interest Rate Swap's notional — the difference is that a Forward is a promise to exchange two real currency amounts on one future date, not a stream of periodic net cash flows calculated off a reference number.

12 · The ISIN-equivalent for derivative products

UPI — Unique Product Identifier: Theory & Worked Example

A global, standardized identifier for an OTC derivative product — the closest thing the derivatives world has to an ISIN, but identifying a product template, not one specific trade.

Deep dive available. This section covers UPI (and UTI) at the level this curriculum needs going forward. For the full picture — UTI's structure and generation waterfall, UPI reference-data templates across all six asset classes, how ANNA-DSB actually operates (including its separate role issuing OTC-derivative ISINs for MiFIR), and a regime-by-regime map of where each identifier is required — see the standalone UPI, UTI & ANNA-DSB Deep Dive.

A UPI is issued by the DSB (Derivatives Service Bureau), a subsidiary of the same organization (ANNA) that coordinates ISIN issuance worldwide, under technical guidance from CPMI-IOSCO. Since roughly 2022–2024, regulators in most major jurisdictions (CFTC, EMIR/UK EMIR rewrites, and others — see the Abbreviations Glossary §09) have made the UPI a mandatory field on OTC derivative trade reports, specifically to replace the patchwork of internal, firm-specific product codes that made it impossible for regulators to compare data across firms.

The critical thing that trips people up: UPI identifies the product, not the trade, and not a specific counterparty pair. This is easiest to see by putting all four identifier types side by side:

IdentifierWhat it identifiesUnique per…Example
ISINA specific cash securityOne instrument, referenced by many tradesUS0378331005 (Apple common stock)
UPIA derivative product template (asset class + type + underlier + key economic terms)One product, referenced by many trades — even across different counterpartiesOne UPI for "5Y CAD Fixed-Float IRS referencing CORRA"
UTIOne specific derivative transactionExactly one trade, generated at executionA brand-new UTI every time Bank Of Toronto executes a swap
Trade IDA firm's own internal transaction referenceExactly one trade, but only meaningful inside that firm's own systemsBOT-2026-000514

Notice the pattern mirrors Section 01's hierarchy almost exactly: UPI sits at roughly the "Product/Instrument" level (reference data, looked up once, reused constantly), while UTI and Trade ID sit at the "Trade" level (a fresh value every single time). The difference from cash instruments is that a derivative doesn't get its own ISIN-like "one instrument, many trades" identifier at the instrument level, because — as Section 07 explained — a derivative has no existence independent of the two parties' contract. UPI fills that structural gap at the product level instead, one rung higher than where an ISIN would sit.

How a UPI is actually built

A UPI is a 12-character code (formatted like an ISIN), but the code itself is just a lookup key — the real content lives in the UPI Reference Data record behind it, a standardized set of fields describing the product: Asset Class, Instrument Type, Underlier(s), Notional Currency, Delivery Type, and a handful of other economic terms specific to that instrument type. Two derivative trades generate the same UPI if and only if every one of those product-defining fields matches — the trade-specific details (notional amount, price/rate, counterparty, trade date) are deliberately not part of what defines a UPI, which is exactly why the same UPI can legitimately appear on thousands of unrelated trades.

Worked example — one UPI, three different Interest Rate Swap trades

Revisiting the 5Y CAD Interest Rate Swap product from Section 08 — suppose Bank Of Toronto executes three separate swaps over a few months, all sharing the same product shape (5Y tenor, CAD notional, fixed-vs-CORRA-floating) but with completely different trade-specific details:

But because all three share the identical product shape (5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swap), they all report the same UPI. If you ever see a reporting query group thousands of distinct trades under one UPI value, that's not a data quality bug — it's the UPI doing exactly what it was designed to do: let a regulator aggregate "how much 5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swap risk exists across the whole market" without having to understand every firm's internal product coding scheme.

13 · The third worked example, alongside CDS and FX Forward

Worked Example — Interest Rate Swap (IRS)

An IRS is an agreement to exchange a fixed stream of interest payments for a floating stream, both calculated off the same Notional, which itself never changes hands. Unlike Section 09's CDS (a contingent payout that may never happen) or Section 10's FX Forward (two real currency amounts that eventually settle), an IRS's notional never settles at all — only a small net difference moves, and it moves every single period, not once at maturity.

Worked Example · 5Y CAD IRSBank Of Toronto ↔ Goldman Sachs
Notional          : CAD 100,000,000  (reference only — never exchanged)
Fixed Rate         : 4.00% (Bank Of Toronto pays)
Floating Rate      : 3-month CORRA + 0.05% spread (Goldman Sachs pays)
Term               : 5 years, quarterly payment dates (20 settlements total)

SCENARIO A — CORRA averages 3.20% this quarter (the normal case)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
  Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 3.25% x (91/365) = CAD 810,068
                                       (3.20% CORRA + 0.05% spread)

  Bank Of Toronto  ---- pays CAD 187,192 net ---->  Goldman Sachs

SCENARIO B — CORRA instead averages 4.30% this quarter (rates moved up)
----------------------------------------------------------
  Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
  Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 4.35% x (91/365) = CAD 1,084,521
                                       (4.30% CORRA + 0.05% spread)

  Goldman Sachs  ---- pays CAD 87,261 net ---->  Bank Of Toronto
  (the direction FLIPS — same trade, same two parties, opposite net payer,
  purely because CORRA reset higher this quarter)

  -> This repeats every quarter for 5 years — 20 separate net settlements,
     each independently determined by that period's own CORRA reset. The
     CAD 100,000,000 notional itself never moves on ANY of those 20 dates.
ISDA classification for this trade: Asset Class = Interest Rate, Base Product = IRSwap, Sub-Product = FixedFloat, Transaction Type = Swap. This is the exact four-level label behind the "Interest Rate / IRSwap / FixedFloat / Swap" shorthand already used in Section 11's cross-reference table.
Takeaway. Put side by side with Sections 09 and 10, this trade completes a genuinely useful three-way contrast in "what actually settles": an IRS's notional never settles (only a net difference, repeatedly); an FX Forward's notional does eventually settle (both full currency amounts, once, Section 10); a CDS's notional may never settle at all (a contingent lump sum, only if a credit event occurs, Section 09). Three OTC derivatives, three completely different relationships between "Notional" and "what actually moves in cash" — exactly the kind of field-level nuance a reporting system has to encode per product, not assume uniformly.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — New market value = CAD 24,625,000
Math · Scaled QuantityCAD 25,000,000
Market Value = 25,000,000 x 98.50 / 100 = CAD 24,625,000

Quantity and Value changed; Price, Currency, Instrument, and all reference-data fields stayed the same. Quantity (Trade field) scales the value directly. Price (98.50) didn't change because it's the market's price for the instrument at that moment, independent of how much any one buyer wants. Everything above the Trade layer — Asset Class, Product, Instrument, ISIN — is identical, because it's still the same bond.

Q2 — More likely a bond

Tickers exist for exchange-traded instruments, which in practice means mainly equities. A CUSIP with no Ticker is the classic signature of a bond record — bonds get identifiers (CUSIP/ISIN) but never trade under an exchange ticker the way shares do.

Q3 — One is Quantity, the other is Notional

The CAD 100,000,000 bond purchase means CAD 100,000,000 face value of bonds actually gets delivered against CAD ~100,000,000 (give or take price) in cash at settlement — real securities and real cash move. The CAD 100,000,000 swap notional never moves at all; it only exists so the fixed and floating legs have a number to multiply against (Section 03, and Module 1 §8). Same displayed figure, completely different settlement reality.

Q4 — Trade is the only layer that's new every time

A single instrument like "Canada 5% Bond 2035" can sit underneath thousands of separate Trade records over its lifetime — every one of those trades still points back to the same Asset Class (Fixed Income), Product (Government Bond), and Instrument (that one bond). Only the Trade layer captures who transacted, how much, at what price, and when.

Q5 — ISIN and CUSIP still work everywhere; Ticker likely would not

ISIN is globally unique by design and travels with the security regardless of which exchange it trades on. CUSIP is also unaffected by exchange venue (it identifies the security, not the listing). Ticker, however, is exchange-specific — Apple's ticker on a European exchange (e.g. Frankfurt's Xetra) is commonly shown as something like APC, not AAPL, because tickers are assigned per listing venue, not globally like ISIN/CUSIP.

Q6 — Payout = CAD 15,000,000
Math · Loss Given DefaultRecovery 25%
Loss Given Default = Notional x (1 - Recovery Rate)
                    = 20,000,000 x (1 - 0.25)
                    = CAD 15,000,000

Recovery Rate represents how many cents on the dollar creditors get back after a default (e.g. from liquidating the defaulted company's remaining assets). Recovery Rate and CDS payout move in opposite directions: at 40% recovery the payout was CAD 12,000,000; dropping recovery to 25% means creditors recover even less of their own money, so the protection seller (Goldman Sachs) has to make up a bigger shortfall — CAD 15,000,000.

Q7 — ISDA classifies contracts, not standalone securities

A bond exists independently of who currently holds it; buying or selling one just transfers ownership of something that already existed (Section 06). An Interest Rate Swap only exists because two counterparties agreed to it — it has no existence outside that bilateral contract, which is exactly the kind of thing ISDA taxonomy was built to classify consistently across the industry (Section 07). Cash instruments use CFI codes and ISIN/CUSIP instead; they were never in ISDA's scope.

Q8 — Asset Class → Base Product → Sub-Product → Transaction Type

Using the Section 09 example: Asset Class = Credit, Base Product = SingleName, Sub-Product = Corporate, Transaction Type = CreditDefaultSwap. "Credit" sits at the first level (Asset Class); "CreditDefaultSwap" sits at the last level (Transaction Type). The taxonomy gets more specific at each level — "Credit" alone doesn't distinguish a CDS from a Total Return Swap, but by the fourth level, "CreditDefaultSwap" is unambiguous.

Q9 — Bad outcome (an opportunity cost) for Bank Of Toronto
Math · Mirror ScenarioSelling USD forward
Bank Of Toronto sells USD 10,000,000 forward at locked rate 1.3545:
  Receives CAD 13,545,000 in 3 months, regardless of where spot lands.

If spot later moves to 1.3700 (USD got MORE expensive):
  Selling USD at that HIGHER spot rate would fetch MORE CAD:
  10,000,000 x 1.3700 = CAD 13,700,000 (spot) vs CAD 13,545,000 (forward)
  -> Forward COST Bank Of Toronto CAD 155,000 vs. waiting and selling at spot.
This is the mirror of the original buyer scenario: in Section 10, Bank Of Toronto (the USD buyer) benefited when spot rose above the locked forward rate, because buying gets cheaper as the forward looks better by comparison. Here, as the USD seller, Bank Of Toronto is hurt when spot rises above the locked forward rate, because selling later at the higher spot would have fetched more CAD. Whether a rate move "helps" or "hurts" a forward always depends on which side of the currency pair you locked in — buyer and seller face exactly opposite exposures to the same rate movement.
Q10 — Their UTI and Trade ID are different
Comparison · Same UPI, Different TradesTrade A vs. Trade C
Trade A: UTI CA1B0T20260817001, Trade ID BOT-2026-000514, Notional CAD 100,000,000, Rate 4.00%, 17-Aug-2026
Trade C: UTI CA1B0T20261014003, Trade ID BOT-2026-000725, Notional CAD 250,000,000, Rate 4.15%, 14-Oct-2026
A UPI is deliberately built only from product-defining fields (Asset Class, Instrument Type, Underlier, Notional Currency, Tenor) — Section 12 lists exactly which fields those are, and counterparty/notional/rate/date are not among them. That's by design: the UPI's job is to let a regulator group "all 5Y CAD fixed-vs-CORRA swaps" together across the whole market, which only works if trade-specific details are excluded from what defines it. UTI (and, internally, Trade ID) exist precisely to carry the trade-specific identity that UPI intentionally leaves out — every real transaction gets a fresh one, even when a thousand other transactions share its exact product template.
Q11 — Net settlement = CAD 0, but that does NOT mean nothing happens on that date
Math · Fixed Equals Floating5Y CAD IRS
Fixed leg (Bank Of Toronto pays)  : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
Floating leg (Goldman Sachs pays) : 100,000,000 x 4.00% x (91/365) = CAD 997,260
                                     (3.95% CORRA + 0.05% spread)
Net = 997,260 - 997,260 = CAD 0
Both legs still get independently calculated off that quarter's CORRA reset — the netting step just happens to produce a zero result this particular quarter, purely by coincidence of where CORRA landed. The trade doesn't pause, terminate, or skip processing; the next quarter's settlement is calculated completely independently, off whatever CORRA does next. A reporting system that only books an entry when net cash actually moves would silently miss recording this period's settlement altogether — the correct behavior is to always compute and report both legs and the net, even when the net happens to equal zero.
