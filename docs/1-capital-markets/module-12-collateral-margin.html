01 · Four modules, one continuous example

What Is Collateral & Margin? — Closing the Chain

Modules 10 and 11 built a chain of numbers that all describe risk without doing anything about it. Collateral is the actual asset posted to cover that risk; Margin is the process of calculating how much is required.

Diagram · The ChainTrade → Valuation → Exposure → Margin → Collateral
Trade  ->  Valuation (Module 10)  ->  Exposure (Module 11)  ->  Margin  ->  Collateral
TermMeaning
CollateralThe actual asset (cash or securities) posted by one party to cover the other's exposure
MarginThe broader process/amount — calculating how much collateral is required
Initial Margin (IM)Collateral posted upfront, sized to cover Potential Future Exposure (Module 11 §2)
Variation Margin (VM)Collateral exchanged periodically to cover today's MTM movement (Module 11 §1)
Cash CollateralCurrency posted directly — needs no valuation adjustment
Securities CollateralA bond or other security posted instead of cash — needs a Haircut (Section 03)
Eligible CollateralThe specific list of asset types a CSA (Module 2 §7) permits as collateral
HaircutA percentage reduction applied to a security's Market Value when used as collateral
Diagram · The Full Chain, With Real NumbersContinuing Module 11 §3's netting set
Module 10: Trade 1 (IRS) MTM = +CAD 1,500,000
           Trade 2 (FX Fwd) MTM = +CAD 900,000
           Trade 3 (CDS) MTM = -CAD 400,000
           (Gross Positive MTM = CAD 2,400,000)

Module 11 §3: NET EXPOSURE (after ISDA netting) = CAD 2,000,000

Module 12 (this module):
           Collateral Held (Section 05 explains the exact number)
             = CAD 1,800,000
           Remaining (uncollateralized) Exposure
             = 2,000,000 - 1,800,000 = CAD 200,000

-> Four modules, one continuous worked example: Bank Of Toronto's
   real, final risk to Goldman Sachs after EVERYTHING -- netting
   AND collateral -- is CAD 200,000, not the CAD 2,400,000 the
   raw MTM numbers alone would suggest.
Takeaway. Every number in this chain is smaller than the one before it, and each reduction happens for a completely different reason — netting (Module 11 §3) is a legal mechanism, collateral is a physical asset actually changing hands. A risk report that jumps straight from MTM to "the bank is fine" without showing this chain is hiding exactly the work that makes that conclusion true.

02 · The future vs the present

Initial Margin vs Variation Margin — Deep Dive

Module 3 §6 introduced IM and VM with illustrative numbers; Module 11 §7 introduced SIMM. This section connects both to why they protect against two completely different failure modes.

Diagram · IM Protects the Future, VM Protects the PresentCAD 100,000,000 IRS, from Module 3 §6
INITIAL MARGIN (IM) — Module 3 §6's worked example:
  100,000,000 x 2.00% (illustrative rate for a 5Y IRS) = CAD 2,000,000
  Posted ONCE, at trade inception, by BOTH sides independently.
  Purpose: if the counterparty defaults TOMORROW, before any VM
  has even had a chance to react, IM is the buffer that covers
  the loss during the time it takes to close out and replace the
  position (the "Margin Period of Risk," typically 10 business
  days for bilateral trades under UMR).

VARIATION MARGIN (VM) — Module 3 §6's worked example:
  Next business day, MTM shifts CAD 180,000 in Bank Of Toronto's
  favor -> Goldman Sachs pays CAD 180,000 VM to Bank Of Toronto
  Purpose: keeps CURRENT Exposure (Module 11 §1) at effectively
  ZERO on an ongoing basis, by transferring exactly today's MTM
  movement, every business day, for the life of the trade.

-> IM is sized off Module 11 §2's PFE (a FORWARD-LOOKING
   estimate); VM is sized off Module 10 §1's MTM (a CURRENT
   snapshot). Two completely different questions, two completely
   different collateral flows, calculated on two different
   schedules (IM once at inception plus periodic recalculation;
   VM daily).
Takeaway. A position can have substantial IM posted and still generate a large VM call the very next day — IM being adequate says nothing about whether VM is current, because IM protects against a future default scenario while VM tracks today's value. Confusing the two is the collateral-management version of Module 10/11's MTM-vs-Exposure confusion, one level further down the chain.

03 · Not all collateral is created equal

Collateral Types and Haircuts

Cash needs no adjustment; securities need a Haircut, because between posting and possible liquidation, the security's own market value could fall.

Math · HaircutCanada 5% Bond 2035 as collateral
Market Value (Module 5 §4a, Module 10 §1): CAD 9,850,000
Haircut (illustrative, for a high-quality government bond): 2%

Collateral Value = Market Value x (1 - Haircut)
                  = 9,850,000 x 0.98
                  = CAD 9,653,000

-> The bond's Collateral Value (CAD 9,653,000) is LOWER than its
   Market Value (CAD 9,850,000) -- the CAD 197,000 gap is the
   buffer against the bond itself losing value before it can be
   sold to cover a default. A riskier, less liquid security (a
   corporate bond, or equity) would carry a MUCH larger haircut
   than a government bond -- Eligible Collateral schedules
   (Section 01) typically list a specific haircut percentage per
   asset type, not one flat rate for everything.
Takeaway. "Collateral Value" is a genuinely distinct field from "Market Value," exactly the same kind of distinction Module 10 §1 established between Price, Market Value, and Fair Value — a reporting system that stores only one number per security and reuses it for both valuation and collateral purposes will silently overstate how much protection a posted security actually provides.

04 · Someone neutral has to hold it

Collateral Movement Mechanics — Pledge, Custodian & Tri-Party

Collateral has to physically move somewhere, and it has to be held by someone neutral enough that both counterparties trust the arrangement.

Diagram · Pledge/Receive and Custody ModelsBilateral custody vs tri-party
PLEDGE: Bank Of Toronto POSTS (pledges) collateral TO Goldman
        Sachs, because Bank Of Toronto owes the exposure.
RECEIVE: Goldman Sachs RECEIVES that same collateral.
        (On the netting set from Section 01, it's actually
        Goldman Sachs who owes Bank Of Toronto CAD 2,000,000 net
        -- so in THIS relationship, Goldman Sachs pledges and
        Bank Of Toronto receives.)

CUSTODIAN vs TRI-PARTY AGENT -- two different operating models:

Bilateral custody: Bank Of Toronto's own custodian (Module 1 §7)
  holds collateral received directly, and Bank Of Toronto's own
  Middle Office values it, applies haircuts, and tracks
  Eligible Collateral rules manually against the CSA.

Tri-party arrangement: a neutral THIRD PARTY (e.g. Euroclear,
  Clearstream, or a custodian bank acting as tri-party agent)
  sits between Bank Of Toronto and Goldman Sachs. Both parties
  agree the Eligible Collateral schedule and haircuts UP FRONT;
  the tri-party agent then automatically selects, values,
  applies haircuts to, and even SUBSTITUTES eligible securities
  from a pool -- neither Bank Of Toronto nor Goldman Sachs has
  to manually pick and track individual securities day to day.

COLLATERAL SUBSTITUTION: if Bank Of Toronto wants to sell a bond
  it's currently using as posted collateral (e.g. to fund
  something else), it can SUBSTITUTE a different eligible asset
  of equivalent Collateral Value -- a tri-party agent handles
  this automatically; bilateral custody requires manual
  negotiation and agreement from the receiving party first.
Takeaway. Tri-party arrangements exist specifically to remove the operational burden of Section 03's haircut math and Eligible Collateral checking from both trading counterparties — a genuinely important IT/operations distinction, because a firm using tri-party custody has fundamentally different system integration needs than one managing bilateral collateral in-house.

05 · Not a mistake — a negotiated term

Remaining Exposure — Closing the Chain

Collateral rarely covers exposure down to exactly zero — a Threshold is a standard CSA term, and it's precisely what explains this module's opening worked example.

Math · Threshold, Required Collateral & Remaining ExposureBank Of Toronto ↔ Goldman Sachs
Net Exposure to Goldman Sachs (Module 11 §3): CAD 2,000,000
CSA Threshold agreed between Bank Of Toronto and Goldman Sachs:
  CAD 200,000 (the amount of exposure Bank Of Toronto has agreed
  to simply ACCEPT, uncollateralized, as the cost of not having
  to call/post tiny amounts of collateral for every small MTM move)

Required Collateral = Net Exposure - Threshold
                     = 2,000,000 - 200,000
                     = CAD 1,800,000  <- exactly Section 01's
                                          "Collateral Held" figure

Remaining (uncollateralized) Exposure = Net Exposure - Collateral Held
                                        = 2,000,000 - 1,800,000
                                        = CAD 200,000  <- exactly
                                          equal to the Threshold

-> This isn't a coincidence -- collateral is called to bring
   Exposure DOWN TO the Threshold, never below it. A Minimum
   Transfer Amount (MTA) works alongside Threshold to prevent
   even that Threshold-sized gap from triggering constant tiny
   collateral movements as MTM wobbles by small amounts day to day.
Takeaway. Remaining Exposure isn't a system malfunction or an under-collateralized mistake — it's the deliberate, negotiated result of the Threshold both counterparties agreed to when they signed the CSA (Module 2 §7). A risk report flagging uncollateralized exposure needs to check the CSA's Threshold term before treating that gap as a problem.

06 · Depends entirely on how it clears

Whether a position even generates an IM/VM requirement at all depends entirely on how it clears (Module 11 §5's bilateral-vs-CCP split), reused here for the margin question specifically.

07 · Static reference data vs daily-computed fields

IT/Reporting Angle — Margin Call Workflow, Static vs Daily Fields, and Disputes

A margin call isn't a single event — it's a multi-step workflow, and getting the field types right is what makes that workflow reliable.

Diagram · Margin Call WorkflowState by state
1. CALLED    — Bank Of Toronto's system computes today's Net
                Exposure (Module 11 §3) and Threshold gap, issues
                a formal margin call to Goldman Sachs for the
                shortfall

2. AGREED / DISPUTED — Goldman Sachs independently recomputes the
                SAME exposure using its OWN valuation (Module 10
                §7's IPV concept applies directly here) -- if the
                two numbers match within an agreed tolerance,
                AGREED; if not, DISPUTED, and the gap gets
                investigated before any collateral moves

3. SETTLED   — once agreed, collateral actually moves (Pledge/
                Receive, Section 04) via the custodian or
                tri-party agent, and both parties' records update

Static vs daily fields: Threshold and Minimum Transfer Amount (Section 05) are static CSA reference data — negotiated once, changed rarely. Net Exposure, Collateral Held, and Remaining Exposure are daily-computed fields — recalculated every business day from that day's MTM. A collateral management system that treats Threshold as if it needs daily recalculation (or, worse, treats today's computed exposure as if it were static reference data) will produce structurally wrong margin calls.

Disputes are normal, not exceptional: because both counterparties independently value the SAME trades, and Module 10 §6 already established that Level 2/3 valuations involve genuine model judgment, small disagreements between two firms' exposure numbers are routine, especially for less liquid, Model-Based positions — a mature collateral operation has a defined dispute-resolution process, not just an assumption that both sides will always agree.
Takeaway. The margin call workflow is where Module 10's valuation-observability concepts (Quote-Based vs Model-Based, Fair Value Levels, IPV) stop being abstract accounting concerns and become an operational, day-to-day reconciliation problem between two counterparties who each compute their own number independently.

08 · Phased in by size, tied to bank liquidity

Regulatory Perspective — UMR Phase-In, BCBS-IOSCO, SFTR & Basel LCR

Collateral management connects directly to a bank's balance-sheet liquidity, not just its counterparty risk.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Collateral & Margin specifically
BCBS-IOSCO Margin FrameworkPhased in over six waves ("Phase 1" through "Phase 6") between 2016 and 2022, based on each firm's Average Aggregate Notional Amount (AANA) of non-cleared derivatives — the largest dealers were brought into scope first; this module's IM requirement (Section 02) only applies to firms actually in scope under their applicable phase
SFTRAlready established (Module 5 §08) as covering repo/securities lending specifically — Section 06's table flags repo as using collateral haircuts directly, and SFTR's reporting obligation covers exactly that collateral alongside the loan's own economics
Basel LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio)A bank must hold enough High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) to survive a 30-day stress scenario; posting collateral (Section 01's chain) TIES UP a bank's own HQLA, while RECEIVING HQLA-eligible collateral can improve the receiving bank's LCR position — collateral management and liquidity management are directly connected balance-sheet concerns
EMIR / Dodd-FrankBoth regimes' Trade Repository reporting include collateral-specific fields — collateral posted, collateral received, and whether a trade is subject to a bilateral CSA or centrally cleared, not just the trade's economics and valuation
CSDR (Central Securities Depositories Regulation, EU)Sets settlement-discipline rules (mandatory buy-ins, cash penalties for settlement fails) that apply directly to securities-collateral movements (Section 04) settling through EU CSDs
Takeaway. UMR's phased rollout is the regulatory mechanism that determines WHETHER Section 02's Initial Margin requirement even applies to a given bilateral relationship — a smaller firm might legitimately have no IM obligation at all if neither party's AANA has crossed the relevant phase's threshold, even though the exact same VM mechanics (and Section 05's Threshold-driven Remaining Exposure) would still apply regardless of IM being in scope.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Required Collateral = CAD 1,500,000. Remaining Exposure = CAD 500,000 (exactly equal to the new Threshold)

Section 05 establishes the exact relationship: Required Collateral = Net Exposure minus Threshold, and Remaining Exposure always equals the Threshold itself once collateral has been called correctly — a larger negotiated Threshold directly produces a larger permanently-uncollateralized exposure gap, by construction, not by error.

Q2 — Collateral Value = 9,850,000 x 0.95 = CAD 9,357,500

A larger haircut percentage produces a larger gap between Market Value and Collateral Value — Section 03 explains this reflects a judgment that this particular security (or this particular CSA's risk appetite) needs a bigger buffer against the collateral itself losing value before it could be liquidated in a default scenario.

Q3 — Without IM, a sudden overnight move (before the next VM cycle) would leave an uncollateralized loss for the entire "Margin Period of Risk"

Section 02's takeaway states this precisely: VM tracks CURRENT exposure day by day, but a counterparty default can happen at any moment, and closing out/replacing a defaulted position takes real time (~10 business days) — IM exists specifically to cover losses that could accumulate during that closeout window, a risk VM structurally cannot address no matter how promptly it's paid.

Q4 — Not necessarily evidence of a broken system — disputes are "normal, not exceptional," especially for Model-Based positions
Section 07's takeaway makes this connection directly: the margin-call dispute process is exactly where Module 10's valuation-observability concepts stop being abstract and become an operational reconciliation problem between two firms that each compute their own number independently — the most likely cause is differing valuation inputs or model assumptions.
Q5 — Repo uses the same haircut mechanic directly on the security exchanged, first established in Module 5 §3

Section 06's takeaway states this explicitly: repo doesn't fit the IM/VM framework because the security and the collateral are the same transaction, not two separate concepts the way a derivative's notional and its posted margin are.

Q6 — Yes — VM under a CSA can apply independently of whether IM is mandated under UMR's AANA-based phase-in

Section 08's takeaway states this directly: "the exact same VM mechanics... would still apply regardless of IM being in scope" — IM and VM aren't a package deal; VM is a much older, more universal CSA convention, while mandatory IM specifically is a newer, AANA-threshold-gated regulatory requirement layered on top for firms large enough to be captured by it.

Q7 — Bilateral custody needs in-house valuation/haircut/substitution logic; tri-party needs a data feed integration to an external agent

Section 04 states this concretely: a firm using tri-party custody needs to integrate with an external agent's systems and trust its calculations, while bilateral custody requires building and maintaining that valuation/haircut/Eligible-Collateral logic in-house — two fundamentally different technical architectures for solving the same underlying Section 03 problem.
