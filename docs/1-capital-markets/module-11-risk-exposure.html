01 · Symmetric vs one-sided

What Is Risk & Exposure? — MTM ≠ Exposure

Module 10 §1 first flagged that Market Value and Exposure are not the same number. This module makes that distinction precise. MTM tells you what a position is worth right now. Exposure tells you how much you stand to lose if your counterparty fails to pay.

TermMeaning
Market Value / MTMThe position's current worth (Module 10 §1) — can be positive OR negative
Current Exposure (CE)The loss you'd suffer TODAY if your counterparty defaulted right now — never negative
Potential Future Exposure (PFE)An estimate of how large Current Exposure could grow by some future date
Net ExposureExposure after netting multiple trades with the same counterparty under one legal agreement (Section 03)
Counterparty RiskThe general risk that the party on the other side of a trade fails to perform its obligations
Credit ExposureOften used interchangeably with Counterparty Risk's dollar amount
Diagram · Why MTM and Exposure DivergeCurrent Exposure = MAX(MTM, 0)
5Y CAD IRS (Module 8 §4a), Bank Of Toronto pays fixed 4.00%:

Scenario A — 6 months later, MTM = +CAD 1,230,000 (Module 10 §4a)
  -> If Goldman Sachs defaulted RIGHT NOW, Bank Of Toronto would
     lose the CAD 1,230,000 it's owed. Current Exposure = CAD 1,230,000.

Scenario B — market instead moved the OTHER way, MTM = -CAD 1,230,000
  (Bank Of Toronto now owes Goldman Sachs, not the reverse)
  -> If Goldman Sachs defaulted RIGHT NOW, Bank Of Toronto loses
     NOTHING from this trade -- Bank Of Toronto still owes the
     money. Current Exposure = CAD 0, even though MTM is very
     much non-zero.

-> A negative MTM is a real number on a P&L report (Module 10 §1)
   but contributes ZERO to Current Exposure. This asymmetry is
   the entire reason the two fields can never be treated as
   synonyms on a risk report.
Takeaway. Exposure is fundamentally a one-sided concept — it only counts the scenarios where you're the one who'd lose money, never the scenarios where your counterparty would. MTM, by contrast, is symmetric: it's exactly as negative for you as it is positive for your counterparty. Confusing the two means either wildly overstating risk or, worse, understating it.

02 · A snapshot vs a forward-looking estimate

Current Exposure vs Potential Future Exposure

Current Exposure answers "what's at risk today." PFE answers "what could be at risk before this trade matures" — and for a long-dated derivative, PFE is very often the larger, more consequential number.

Diagram · A Zero-MTM Trade Still Carries Real Risk5Y CAD IRS at inception
5Y CAD IRS, Notional CAD 100,000,000, AT INCEPTION:
  MTM = 0 (Module 10 §4a — a fairly-priced swap starts at zero)
  Current Exposure = MAX(0, 0) = CAD 0

-> A risk report showing "Current Exposure: CAD 0" on trade date
   is CORRECT, not a broken feed. But it does NOT mean this trade
   is risk-free -- over the next 5 years, rates can move a great
   deal, and Module 10 §4a already showed a 30bp move alone
   producing roughly CAD 1,230,000 of MTM swing after just 6 months.

POTENTIAL FUTURE EXPOSURE (illustrative, using a standard
percentile-of-simulated-outcomes approach real risk systems use):
  At a 95% confidence level, 2 years into the trade, PFE might be
  estimated at roughly CAD 4,500,000 -- the swap's notional,
  remaining term, and rate volatility all feed this number, not
  just today's zero MTM.

-> PFE is why a bank extends a credit limit to a counterparty
   BEFORE trading with them, sized off the WORST plausible future
   exposure, not today's (currently zero) exposure.
Takeaway. Current Exposure is a snapshot; PFE is a forward-looking estimate that has to account for everything that could still happen to a trade's value before it matures — which is exactly why PFE, not Current Exposure, is the number that drives how much Initial Margin gets posted (Module 12) and how large a credit limit a bank extends to a given counterparty in the first place.

03 · One Master Agreement, one net number

Netting — Why Gross Exposure Overstates the Real Risk

Module 2 §7 introduced the ISDA Master Agreement. Its single most consequential feature for risk purposes: close-out netting — every trade under one Master Agreement collapses into a SINGLE net obligation on default, not a pile of separate winners and losers.

Diagram · Gross vs Net ExposureOne ISDA Master Agreement, three trades
Trade 1 — 5Y CAD IRS (Module 8 §4a):        MTM = +CAD 1,500,000
Trade 2 — FX Forward (Module 6 §4b):         MTM = +CAD   900,000
Trade 3 — CDS, Goldman Sachs as protection
          seller (Module 9 §4a):              MTM = -CAD   400,000

GROSS POSITIVE EXPOSURE (sum only the trades where Bank Of
Toronto is owed money -- i.e., pretend netting doesn't exist):
  1,500,000 + 900,000 = CAD 2,400,000

NET EXPOSURE (sum ALL three MTMs together, exactly what close-out
netting under the ISDA Master Agreement actually produces if
Goldman Sachs defaults):
  1,500,000 + 900,000 - 400,000 = CAD 2,000,000

-> Without netting, a risk system would overstate Bank Of
   Toronto's exposure to Goldman Sachs by CAD 400,000 -- exactly
   the amount of Trade 3, which nets AGAINST the other two rather
   than adding on top of them.
Callout — a different scenario than Module 10 §4c. Module 10 §4c revalues this exact same CDS under a scenario where Northstar Resources Inc.'s spread WIDENS, producing a GAIN to Bank Of Toronto's protection position. Here, Trade 3's -CAD 400,000 assumes the opposite illustrative scenario — Northstar Resources Inc.'s spread has instead NARROWED from its original level, leaving Bank Of Toronto's locked-in protection worth less than current market pricing would provide: a loss, not a gain. These are two independent snapshots of the same trade under different market moves, not a contradiction. Separately, the CDS's own notional is USD 10,000,000 (Module 9 §4a); Trade 3's MTM is shown here in CAD because a netting set's exposure is expressed in one common reporting currency across every trade under the Master Agreement, regardless of each trade's own underlying currency.
IT/reporting angle: this is why exposure has to be calculated and stored per legal netting set (all trades under one ISDA Master Agreement with one counterparty), not per individual trade, and not blindly summed across every counterparty a bank trades with. A trade record's "Netting Set ID" or "Master Agreement ID" field is what tells a risk system which other trades' MTMs it's legally allowed to offset against this one.
Takeaway. Netting is the single biggest lever reducing real counterparty risk in the OTC derivatives market without a single trade changing hands — it's a pure legal/contractual mechanism, not a market or pricing effect, and it's exactly why the ISDA Master Agreement is the document a credit risk team cares about most.

04 · Exposure × probability × severity

Counterparty Credit Risk Deep Dive — CVA and Wrong-Way Risk

Module 10 §8 already defined CVA as an adjustment reflecting counterparty default risk. Here's the mechanism: CVA translates Net Exposure and a counterparty's own creditworthiness into a single dollar charge.

Math · CVABank Of Toronto's exposure to Goldman Sachs
Net Exposure to Goldman Sachs (Section 03): CAD 2,000,000
Goldman Sachs' estimated 1-year Probability of Default: 0.50%
Loss Given Default (i.e., 1 - Recovery Rate, Module 9 §1's
Recovery Rate concept applied to a COUNTERPARTY rather than a
CDS Reference Entity): 60%

Simplified CVA ≈ Net Exposure x Probability of Default x Loss
                  Given Default
                = 2,000,000 x 0.50% x 60%
                = CAD 6,000

-> This CAD 6,000 is subtracted from the position's Fair Value
   (Module 10 §1) specifically because the counterparty MIGHT
   default -- a real accounting adjustment, not just an internal
   risk metric.

Wrong-Way Risk: the specific, dangerous case where a counterparty's likelihood of default is correlated with the exposure itself getting larger — Module 9's Q4 answer already previewed this exact scenario: Goldman Sachs selling CDS protection on its own default risk. If Goldman Sachs starts to fail, Bank Of Toronto's exposure to that CDS position grows (Section 02's PFE effect, credit deteriorating) at the exact same moment Goldman Sachs' ability to pay is disappearing — the worst possible correlation a risk model can face.

Takeaway. CVA turns Sections 01-03's Current/Net Exposure concepts into an actual dollar cost embedded in a position's value; Wrong-Way Risk is the reminder that the probability half of that calculation isn't always a fixed, independent number — sometimes it moves in lockstep with the very exposure it's being multiplied against.

05 · Redirected, not eliminated

Cross-Asset-Class Exposure Reference — Bilateral vs CCP

Section 7 in every asset-class module already established which products are bilateral and which are centrally cleared. That same split determines WHO bears the counterparty risk this module has been calculating.

Asset Class / ProductCounterparty Risk Sits With...Why
Cash Equity / Bonds (mostly)CCP (NSCC, CDCC) for equities; bilateral for most bondsModule 4 §7, Module 5 §7
FX Spot/Forward/SwapBilateral, mitigated by CLS's PvP (settlement risk, not counterparty risk)Module 6 §5, §7
Standardized IRSCCP (LCH SwapClear) — Sections 03-04's netting/CVA math still applies, just with the CCP as counterpartyModule 8 §7
FRA / Cap-Floor / Swaption / IR Forward DebtBilateral (mostly)Module 8 §7
CDS Index / liquid Single-Name CDSCCP (ICE Clear Credit, LCH CDSClear)Module 9 §7
Bespoke Single-Name CDSBilateral — Section 04's CVA/wrong-way-risk math applies directlyModule 9 §7
Futures / Listed OptionsAlways CCP, from executionModule 15 §7
Diagram · CCP Exposure Isn't ZeroIt's redirected
Bilateral trade: Bank Of Toronto's counterparty risk -> Goldman
                  Sachs directly. CVA math (Section 04) applies
                  to Goldman Sachs' own creditworthiness.

Centrally cleared trade: Bank Of Toronto's counterparty risk ->
                  the CCP (e.g. LCH SwapClear). Novation (Module
                  3 §6) legally substitutes the CCP for Goldman
                  Sachs -- but Bank Of Toronto STILL has residual
                  exposure to the CCP itself failing, mitigated
                  by the CCP's own layered "default waterfall":
                  the defaulting member's own Initial Margin,
                  then its Default Fund contribution, then other
                  members' mutualized Default Fund contributions,
                  then the CCP's own capital.
Takeaway. Central clearing doesn't make counterparty risk disappear — it concentrates and mutualizes it inside one entity (the CCP) specifically structured, capitalized, and regulated to absorb member defaults, a fundamentally different (and generally safer, for standardized products) risk profile than a web of bilateral exposures, but not literally "zero risk."

06 · The 99th-percentile question

Portfolio-Level Risk — Value at Risk (VaR)

Module 10 §8 already defined VaR as a statistical estimate of maximum expected loss over a time horizon at a confidence level. Here's the calculation, reusing Module 10 §3's bond DV01.

Math · 1-Day 99% VaRCanada 5% Bond 2035 Position
DV01: CAD 7,092 per basis point (Module 10 §3)
Assumed daily yield volatility (1 standard deviation): 8 basis points
99% confidence level ≈ 2.33 standard deviations (a standard
statistical constant for a normal distribution's 99th percentile)

1-Day 99% VaR = DV01 x Confidence Multiplier x Daily Volatility (bps)
              = 7,092 x 2.33 x 8
              = CAD 132,235

-> Interpretation: on 99 out of 100 trading days, this bond
   position should NOT lose more than approximately CAD 132,235
   in a single day, based purely on interest rate moves. On the
   1-in-100 "tail" day, it could lose more -- VaR explicitly does
   NOT bound the worst case, only the 99th-percentile case.
IT/reporting angle: a single position's VaR is a useful sensitivity check, but real risk systems compute portfolio-level VaR across every position a desk (or the whole bank) holds — and because different positions' value changes aren't perfectly correlated, portfolio VaR is almost always less than the simple sum of every individual position's VaR. That diversification benefit is a genuine, calculable number, exactly why risk systems need full correlation data across every asset class in this roadmap, not just each position's own volatility.
Takeaway. VaR complements, but doesn't replace, Sections 01-05's counterparty-specific Exposure/CVA framework — VaR measures how much a position's own value could move against the market, while Exposure and CVA measure how much you'd lose if a specific counterparty failed to pay. A portfolio can have low VaR and still carry dangerous concentrated counterparty exposure to one name, or vice versa.

07 · Six fields, two cadences

IT/Reporting Angle — Exposure Fields, SIMM, and Real-Time vs End-of-Day Risk

Three practical system-design realities this module's concepts create.

Exposure fields aren't Valuation fields: Module 10 §7 already separated Price/Market Value/Unrealized P&L as three distinct fields. Add three more here — Current Exposure, PFE, and Net Exposure (per netting set) — and none of them should be derived by a downstream consumer guessing from Market Value alone; Current Exposure specifically requires the MAX(MTM, 0) floor from Section 01, which a naive "just show me the MTM" report will get wrong on every trade with negative MTM.

SIMM (ISDA Standard Initial Margin Model): a standardized, industry-wide model (built by ISDA, the same body from Module 2 §7's Master Agreement) that calculates Initial Margin for non-centrally-cleared derivatives directly from a position's risk sensitivities (its DV01-like Greeks, per risk factor) rather than a simple percentage-of-notional estimate — the full mechanics belong to Module 12 (Collateral & Margin), but SIMM is fundamentally a PFE-family calculation (Section 02), reused for a margin-sizing purpose instead of a pure risk-reporting one.

Real-time vs end-of-day risk: a Front Office trading desk needs pre-trade risk limit checks in milliseconds — necessarily a simplified, fast approximation. Overnight, a full portfolio revaluation recomputes VaR, PFE, and CVA precisely, incorporating every position and full correlation data. These two numbers can legitimately disagree for the same portfolio at different points in the day, and a reporting system that doesn't clearly label which risk run a number came from will cause genuine confusion when they don't match.

Takeaway. Every concept in this module — Current Exposure, PFE, Net Exposure, CVA, VaR — needs its own clearly-labeled field, computed on its own cadence, because collapsing them into a single "Risk" number for a trade record destroys exactly the information this module spent seven sections building up.

08 · Market risk regulation meets counterparty risk regulation

Regulatory Perspective — SA-CCR, FRTB, CCAR & Uncleared Margin Rules

Two genuinely different regulatory concerns meet here for the first time.

Regulation / BodyHow it touches Risk & Exposure specifically
BCBS SA-CCRThe current Basel methodology banks use to calculate regulatory capital against counterparty credit risk on derivatives — directly formalizes Sections 01-03's Current Exposure/PFE/netting concepts into a regulator-mandated formula, replacing the older, cruder "Current Exposure Method"
BCBS FRTBAlready introduced in Module 10 §8 for its valuation-observability capital charge; it separately also sets capital requirements for market risk (VaR-family measures, Section 06) on the trading book — the same standard covers two different risk dimensions this roadmap has now met in two different modules
Federal Reserve CCARThe US Federal Reserve's annual stress-testing regime for large banks — forces institutions to model portfolio-level losses (Section 06's VaR logic, extended to extreme hypothetical scenarios) and hold enough capital to survive them, a US-specific supervisory exercise without a direct EU/UK equivalent under the same name
EMIR / Dodd-FrankBoth regimes require OTC derivative reports to a Trade Repository to include exposure-related fields alongside valuation fields (Module 10 §08) — Current Exposure and collateral posted are frequently among the mandatory reportable fields
IOSCO / BCBS Uncleared Margin Rules (UMR)The joint framework requiring Initial and Variation Margin on non-centrally-cleared derivatives above certain thresholds — directly consumes this module's PFE (Section 02) and SIMM (Section 07) concepts as its calculation inputs, with the full posting mechanics covered in Module 12
Takeaway. This module's regulatory section is where market-risk regulation (FRTB, CCAR) and counterparty-credit-risk regulation (SA-CCR, UMR) meet — two genuinely different regulatory concerns that both ultimately trace back to the same underlying question this module opened with: how much could actually be lost, and under what circumstances, from a position whose Market Value alone (Module 10) never tells the whole story.

Self-check · Solutions

Recap Quiz — Answers & Explanations

Check your work against these before moving on.

Q1 — Current Exposure = MAX(0, 0) = CAD 0. This does NOT mean zero risk

Section 02's takeaway states this directly: "a risk report showing 'Current Exposure: CAD 0' on trade date is CORRECT, not a broken feed... it does NOT mean this trade is risk-free." PFE, not Current Exposure, is the number a bank should size credit limits against for exactly this reason.

Q2 — Net Exposure = 1,500,000 + 900,000 - 2,600,000 = -CAD 200,000. A negative number means Bank Of Toronto would OWE Goldman Sachs money on close-out

This is Section 01's Current Exposure logic applied at the netting-set level instead of the single-trade level — a negative net number contributes zero to Current Exposure, because Bank Of Toronto isn't the party who stands to lose if Goldman Sachs defaults in this scenario; Bank Of Toronto is the net debtor.

Q3 — New CVA = 2,000,000 x 1.00% x 60% = CAD 12,000 — exactly double the original CAD 6,000

Section 04's CVA formula is a simple product of three factors, so doubling any single input while holding the other two constant doubles the result linearly. This linearity is exactly why Wrong-Way Risk is so dangerous: it's not just that Probability of Default can rise, it's that it can rise AT THE SAME TIME Net Exposure rises too, compounding rather than just doubling the CVA impact.

Q4 — Residual exposure to the CCP itself failing, mitigated but not eliminated by the CCP's default waterfall
Section 05's diagram states this precisely: novation legally substitutes the CCP for the original bilateral counterparty, but Bank Of Toronto's counterparty risk simply moves to a new, differently-structured counterparty rather than vanishing — concentrated and mutualized, but "not literally zero risk."
Q5 — New 1-Day 99% VaR = 7,092 x 2.33 x 16 = CAD 264,470 — exactly double the original

Section 06's VaR formula scales linearly with daily volatility (holding DV01 and the confidence multiplier constant), the same linear-scaling property Q3's CVA answer demonstrated for a different formula.

Q6 — No, not necessarily a system bug

Section 07 explicitly states these two numbers "can legitimately disagree" because they're computed on different cadences with different levels of precision: real-time risk uses fast, simplified approximations, while end-of-day risk recomputes precisely with full portfolio and correlation data. The real risk is a system failing to clearly LABEL which risk run a number came from — that's what causes genuine, avoidable confusion.

Q7 — Different questions: SA-CCR measures counterparty credit risk capital; CCAR measures whole-bank survival under macroeconomic stress

Section 08 distinguishes these directly: SA-CCR formalizes this module's Current Exposure/PFE/netting concepts (Sections 01-03) into a capital formula, while CCAR is a supervisory stress-testing exercise extending Section 06's portfolio-risk logic to hypothetical extreme scenarios — related in spirit (both ultimately protect against a bank failing) but measuring genuinely different things at genuinely different scopes.
